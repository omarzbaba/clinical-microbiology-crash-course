Today’s goal

Learn to read what the lab is telling you — and to tell infection apart from colonization and contamination.

You already know sepsis, pneumonia and urinary tract infection clinically. We’re adding the laboratory framework, the vocabulary, and the prescribing reflexes that sit on top.

Pillar 01 · Specimens & the diagnostic toolbox

The specimen decides the answer.

Clinical microbiology can only answer the question your specimen allows. Start here: how the lab works, what each test can and cannot tell you, and the single distinction that runs through the whole specialty — contamination, colonization, or true infection.

Everything the microbiology laboratory tells you is downstream of one decision made at the bedside: what you collected, how you collected it, and when. The lab is very good at answering questions — but it can only answer the question the specimen allows. This pillar walks from the specimen cup to the final report.

What the clinical microbiology lab actually does

1. Detect

Is an organism there at all?

Direct stains, culture, antigen tests, and nucleic acid amplification all answer the same first question: is there something here? Each method has a floor below which it sees nothing, so a negative result always means “below the limit of this test on this specimen,” not “no infection.”

Fastest signalGram stain, minutes
Most definitiveCulture, days

2. Identify

What organism is it?

Once something grows, identification to genus and species is now usually fast — matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) can name a colony in minutes. Identification alone often changes management, because organism identity predicts likely resistance and likely source.

DrivesEmpiric narrowing, source hunt

3. Test susceptibility

Will the drug work in the tube?

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing reports a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) — the lowest drug concentration that stops visible growth — interpreted as susceptible, intermediate, susceptible-dose-dependent (a separate category used for certain drug-organism pairs), or resistant against breakpoints set by standards bodies such as the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI).

ExampleCefazolin MIC 1 mcg/mL — S
Why it matters

Those three jobs happen in sequence, not in parallel. You cannot get a susceptibility result before you have an isolate, and you cannot get an isolate before something grows. That is the entire reason empiric therapy exists: the first 48 hours of antibiotics are almost always chosen without the answer.

Pre-analytics: the specimen decides the answer

Most bad microbiology results are not laboratory errors. They are collection errors that the laboratory then faithfully reports.

  • Right specimen. Sample the infected compartment, not the surface above it. Tissue and aspirated fluid outperform swabs almost everywhere in the body.
  • Right container. Anaerobic transport for deep collections and abscesses; blood culture bottles for blood (and, in many labs, for joint and peritoneal fluid); a sterile cup, not a swab, for pus.
  • Right volume. A scant sample is a negative result waiting to happen. In blood cultures this is the single biggest lever you control.
  • Right timing. Collect before the first dose of antibiotics whenever the patient’s stability allows. In sepsis this means draw cultures and give antibiotics — not draw cultures, wait, then give antibiotics.
  • Right transport. Delays let contaminating flora overgrow and fastidious organisms die. Urine must be refrigerated or preserved promptly; cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for bacterial culture should go straight to the lab and is generally not refrigerated.
  • Right label and right question. Write the actual source (“left foot ulcer, deep tissue after debridement”), not “wound.” Some organisms are only sought if you ask — the lab does not automatically look for everything.

Two sets from two separate venipuncture sites, each set usually one aerobic and one anaerobic bottle. Disinfect the skin and let it dry, and disinfect the bottle tops too. Most adult bottles are designed for roughly 8 to 10 mL each, so two proper sets is on the order of 40 mL of blood — that volume, more than any other variable, determines whether you find the organism.

Pitfall

The swab is the default because it is what is on the supply cart, not because it is good. A swab holds a tiny volume, dries out, is poor for anaerobes, and preferentially samples surface flora. If you can aspirate it or biopsy it, do not swab it.

Contamination, colonization, infection

If you take one idea from this rotation, take this one. The laboratory reports what grew. Only the clinician can say what it means, and the answer is almost always one of these three.

Contamination

The organism was never in the patient’s compartment

It entered the specimen during collection or processing — off the skin, off the mouth, off a glove. It tells you about technique, not about disease.

ClassicCoagulase-negative staph in 1 of 4 bottles
ClassicMixed skin flora from a superficial swab
ActionUsually none; consider redraw if truly uncertain

Colonization

Really present, really not causing this illness

The organism genuinely lives at that body site without invading. Colonization is normal biology, and treating it does harm without benefit.

ClassicCandida in sputum or in a catheterized urine
ClassicS. aureus on a nares screening swab
ClassicBacteriuria in a patient with no urinary symptoms

Infection

Organism plus host response plus a syndrome

Invasion with tissue damage and an inflammatory response that matches the clinical picture. Notice that two of the three ingredients are clinical, not microbiologic.

SupportingNeutrophils on the stain, from a sterile site
SupportingPure heavy growth, multiple positive sets
SupportingSyndrome fits the organism
Do not confuse

Candida growing from a respiratory specimen is essentially never Candida pneumonia in an immunocompetent adult — it is oropharyngeal colonization. In contrast, Candida growing from a blood culture is never dismissed: candidemia demands source control, line evaluation, an ophthalmologic examination in most protocols, and infectious diseases input. Same genus, opposite implications, entirely because of the compartment it came from.

Student pearl

Before you look at a positive result, finish this sentence: “If this organism is real, this patient should have …” Then look at the patient. If the syndrome is absent, you are probably looking at contamination or colonization, no matter how impressive the report looks.

The Gram stain: the fastest information in microbiology

Four reagents, about a minute of bench time, and a result that can reach you within an hour of the specimen arriving. Crystal violet and iodine stain everything, alcohol decolorizes, safranin counterstains what lost the purple. Organisms with thick peptidoglycan hold the purple and are called Gram-positive; those with thin peptidoglycan and an outer membrane wash out and take up the pink counterstain, and are called Gram-negative.

Purple = Gram-positive. Pink or red = Gram-negative. That one word already splits your empiric antibiotic thinking in half. Some clinically important organisms are invisible or unreliable on Gram stain: mycobacteria (waxy cell wall, need an acid-fast stain), Mycoplasma (no cell wall at all), Legionella, Treponema, and Chlamydia. A stain reading “no organisms seen” never excludes these.

Detection floorRoughly 100,000 organisms per mL to be visible

Cocci are round, rods (also called bacilli) are elongated, and coccobacilli are the awkward middle. Also reported: curved or spiral forms, branching filamentous forms (think Nocardia and Actinomyces), and fungal elements — budding yeast, and pseudohyphae, which suggest Candida species.

Gram-negative rods in bloodThink Enterobacterales, Pseudomonas

How the organisms sit together is a genuine clue, because it reflects the plane of cell division.

These are strong hypotheses, not identifications. The culture still has to confirm.

The stain reports the patient’s cells too, and they are often more informative than the bacteria. Neutrophils mean there is an inflammatory response at that site. Squamous epithelial cells mean the specimen touched a mucosal surface — on a sputum specimen, they mean mouth.

Many laboratories screen expectorated sputum this way and will reject or cancel a specimen with abundant squamous cells, because culturing it would only report the patient’s oral flora. Exact thresholds and which specimen types are screened vary by institution, and lower respiratory specimens such as BAL, and specimens for mycobacterial or fungal work, are typically handled under different rules.

Clinical pattern

A CSF Gram stain showing Gram-positive lancet-shaped diplococci in a febrile adult with neck stiffness is enough to commit to treating pneumococcal meningitis right now — hours before any culture. That is the whole value proposition of the Gram stain: it is not precise, but it arrives while the decision is still open.

Pitfall

A rejected sputum specimen is not the lab being obstructive. It is the lab preventing a report that would otherwise be quoted on rounds as evidence of pneumonia. Recollect with coaching, or escalate to a deeper specimen — do not resend the same saliva.

Culture: still the reference standard, and still slow

Media, in plain terms

Solid, broth, selective, differential

Solid agar plates let a single organism grow into a visible colony you can pick, count, and test — that is why plates dominate. Broth is more sensitive for small numbers of organisms but gives you no colony and no quantitation, so a broth-only positive is treated cautiously.

Selective media contain something that suppresses organisms you do not want — MacConkey agar contains bile salts and crystal violet, which hold back Gram-positives so Gram-negative rods stand out. Differential media make organisms look different from each other: lactose fermenters turn pink on MacConkey, and hemolysis patterns on blood agar separate streptococci. Chocolate agar is blood agar with lysed red cells, which releases the growth factors that fastidious organisms such as Haemophilus require.

Student pearl

Selective answers “who is allowed to grow.” Differential answers “who looks like what once they do.” A single plate can be both.

Atmosphere and incubation

Aerobic, anaerobic, and the long holds

Routine cultures are incubated in air, often with added carbon dioxide. Anaerobes need oxygen-free conditions from the moment of collection through incubation, which is why anaerobic transport medium matters and why a dried-out swab is close to useless for an abscess.

Some cultures are set up only when requested: fungal, mycobacterial, and extended-hold cultures for slow organisms. If you suspect them, say so on the order — a standard bacterial culture will be discarded long before they would have grown.

Pitfall

“No growth at 24 hours” is a status update, not a negative result. Stopping antibiotics on a one-day preliminary in a sick patient is one of the more common and more dangerous misreadings of a micro report. Wait for the word final, and remember that prior antibiotics can sterilize a culture without curing the patient.

Blood cultures done properly

Blood cultures are the highest-stakes specimen in the hospital and the one most often collected badly. Two variables account for most of the performance: how much blood, and how clean the draw.

Volume is the sensitivity lever

In adults, nothing else comes close

Bacteremia in adults is often low-density — a small number of organisms per milliliter of blood. Because of that, yield rises with the volume cultured in a roughly proportional way. Two full sets sample far more blood than one, and an underfilled bottle quietly costs sensitivity that no downstream technology can recover.

Adult target2 sets, all bottles filled to the fill line
Approximate total~40 mL across 4 bottles
Why it matters

If you remember one number from this pillar, remember that the amount of blood in the bottle matters more than the timing of the fever spike.

Two sets, two sites, before antibiotics

Interpretability, not superstition

Separate venipuncture sites give independent draws, so an organism appearing in both is far harder to explain as skin contamination. Two sets also double the volume. And a single dose of an effective antibiotic can render cultures negative within hours, which is why the goal in suspected sepsis is to draw and treat in the same few minutes rather than to delay either one.

Interpreting a positive set: the organism’s identity and the proportion of bottles positive do most of the interpretive work.

  • Coagulase-negative staphylococci in 1 of 4 bottles in a patient without hardware and without a compatible syndrome: usually contamination. It is the commonest skin organism and the commonest blood culture contaminant.
  • The same coagulase-negative staphylococcus in 4 of 4 bottles, especially with a prosthetic valve, prosthetic joint, or an indwelling intravascular device: treat this as real until proven otherwise.
  • Essentially never dismissed as contaminants: Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Streptococcus pyogenes, Enterobacterales, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Listeria, and any Candida species. A single positive bottle with any of these needs a full evaluation.
  • Other common contaminants — Corynebacterium species, Cutibacterium acnes, Bacillus species other than B. anthracis, viridans group streptococci in a single bottle — are usually contaminants, but each has a real disease it can cause in the right host, so read them against the clinical picture rather than by reflex.

A blood culture just flagged positive — what happens next

1
Hour 0 — the instrument flags the bottle

Automated systems continuously monitor each bottle for the metabolic signature of growth and alarm the moment a bottle turns positive, day or night.

2
Within about an hour — Gram stain, phoned as a critical result

A drop from the bottle is stained and read immediately. Almost every laboratory treats a positive blood culture Gram stain as a critical value that must be communicated directly to the clinical team. The bottle is also subcultured to plates.

3
Hour 1–2 — rapid molecular identification, where available

Many laboratories run a blood culture identification panel straight off the positive bottle. Within an hour or two this can return a species-level identification plus selected resistance markers — for example, genes associated with methicillin resistance in staphylococci, vancomycin resistance in enterococci, or common carbapenemases. Availability and panel content differ substantially between institutions.

Real bacteremia — act now

Reassess empiric coverage against the organism class, hunt for the source, obtain imaging if indicated, remove or evaluate intravascular lines, and consider infectious diseases consultation. For S. aureus bacteremia and for candidemia, formal consultation and structured follow-up are the standard of care in most centers, and follow-up blood cultures to document clearance are expected.

Probable contaminant — do not reflex to vancomycin

One bottle of a skin organism in a well patient with no hardware: reassess the patient, review whether the draw was from a line, and in most cases follow clinically rather than starting therapy. Unnecessary treatment of a contaminant means days of an intravenous drug, line risk, and toxicity for no benefit.

4
Hour 12–24 — colonies grow; identification confirmed

Once there are colonies on the plate, MALDI-TOF confirms species identification in minutes, and the isolate is set up for susceptibility testing.

5
Day 2–3 — susceptibilities return; therapy is narrowed

MICs and their interpretations arrive. This is the moment the patient should move from broad empiric coverage to the narrowest effective agent — the single highest-value stewardship action on any given day.

Advanced pearl

Time to positivity is a supporting clue. True bacteremia with virulent organisms tends to flag early, often within the first day, because the inoculum is higher; contaminants introduced from skin tend to flag later. The idea is used formally in suspected catheter-related bloodstream infection: when paired catheter and peripheral cultures are drawn simultaneously, the catheter-drawn bottle turning positive substantially earlier than the peripheral one — a commonly used threshold is about two hours — supports the line as the source. Treat this as one input among several, not as a rule that overrides the clinical picture.

Rapid identification: mass spectrometry and molecular panels

MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry

Names the colony in minutes — and only that

A colony is spotted onto a target plate, hit with a laser, and the resulting spectrum of mostly ribosomal proteins is compared against a reference database. The output is a species identification, typically in minutes. This technology is the single biggest reason identification stopped being the slow step in the laboratory.

What it needsSomething that already grew
What it givesGenus and species
What it does not giveAny susceptibility result
Do not confuse

“The lab identified it this morning” does not mean susceptibilities are available. Identification and susceptibility are separate steps on separate clocks, and only the second one tells you whether your antibiotic works.

Multiplex PCR panels

Fast, sensitive, and narrow by design

Nucleic acid amplification panels test a specimen for a fixed menu of targets at once, usually in about an hour. The families you will meet:

Blood culture ID panelOff a positive bottle; organism plus resistance genes
Respiratory panelViruses plus a few atypical bacteria
Meningitis/encephalitis panelCSF; common bacterial, viral, and cryptococcal targets
Gastrointestinal panelBacterial, viral, and parasitic enteric targets

The CSF panel is meant to supplement, not replace, CSF culture and cryptococcal antigen testing; both false negatives and false positives have been described, and a negative panel in a patient who looks like meningitis does not stop treatment.

Pitfall

PCR detects nucleic acid, not life and not disease. That means it can stay positive after the organism is dead, it can detect an organism that is merely colonizing, and it will never report anything that is not on the panel. The two clinical consequences you will see most often: a gastrointestinal panel positive for a target that is not causing this patient’s symptoms, and a positive C. difficile nucleic acid test in a colonized patient whose diarrhea is from laxatives. A panel result is a finding to interpret, not a diagnosis.

Advanced pearl

MALDI-TOF has known blind spots where closely related organisms share nearly identical protein spectra. Shigella species are not reliably separated from Escherichia coli, and Streptococcus pneumoniae can be difficult to distinguish from closely related viridans group streptococci such as S. mitis. Laboratories handle these with confirmatory phenotypic or molecular testing, which is one reason a report may say “identification pending confirmation” on an organism the instrument technically already named.

The rest of the toolbox

Antigen tests

Fast, narrow, useful when positive

These detect a specific microbial molecule directly. Pneumococcal and Legionella urinary antigen tests can support a pneumonia diagnosis within the hour, and cryptococcal antigen on serum or CSF is a sensitive and central test for cryptococcal disease.

Key limitationLegionella urinary antigen detects serogroup 1 only
Key limitationCan remain positive after treatment

Serology

Measures the host, not the organism

Antibody testing tells you about exposure and immune response. It is often retrospective — a meaningful answer may require paired acute and convalescent sera weeks apart — and IgM assays are prone to cross-reactivity. Serology is valuable for organisms that are hard or hazardous to culture, and generally poor for diagnosing acute bacterial infection at the bedside.

Best useExposure history, hard-to-culture organisms

Acid-fast stain

For mycobacteria

Mycobacteria resist Gram staining because of their lipid-rich cell wall, so they are stained by acid-fast methods, including fluorescent techniques. Smear is fast but considerably less sensitive than culture, and a positive smear does not by itself distinguish M. tuberculosis from nontuberculous mycobacteria — molecular testing and culture answer that.

RememberA negative smear does not exclude tuberculosis

Fungal stains

KOH and friends

Potassium hydroxide preparations dissolve host material so fungal elements stand out, and fluorescent brighteners make them easier still. Histopathologic stains on tissue answer a question culture cannot: whether the fungus is actually invading, rather than sitting on a surface.

Culture saysWhat it is
Histopathology saysWhether it is invading

16S rRNA gene sequencing

The culture-negative send-out

Broad-range amplification and sequencing of a conserved bacterial gene can identify an organism from a sterile-site specimen that never grew — classically a heart valve, bone, or joint fluid from a patient already on antibiotics. It is usually a reference laboratory test taking days.

LimitsContamination-prone; weak for polymicrobial samples
LimitsNo susceptibility information

Susceptibility testing

Where the MIC comes from

Automated broth microdilution and gradient diffusion strips report an MIC directly; disk diffusion instead reports a zone-of-inhibition diameter interpreted as S/I/R without generating an MIC. All three measure whether a standardized inoculum grows in the presence of drug. The MIC is then interpreted against breakpoints, which are periodically revised by standards bodies such as CLSI and EUCAST — meaning the same MIC can be called susceptible in one year and not in another.

When not to test: diagnostic stewardship

Antimicrobial stewardship gets the attention, but most unnecessary antibiotics start with an unnecessary test. A test ordered without a question generates a result without a meaning, and someone will treat it.

  • No urine culture without urinary symptoms. Asymptomatic bacteriuria is common, especially in older adults and anyone with a catheter, and treating it does not help. The well-established exceptions are pregnancy and before urologic procedures expected to cause mucosal bleeding. Fever alone in a patient with another plausible source is not a urinary symptom.
  • No C. difficile testing on formed stool. If it holds its shape, the test cannot be interpreted. Equally, do not test a patient who received laxatives or stool softeners in the preceding day or two — you will find colonization and call it disease. Many laboratories enforce this with hard specimen-rejection rules.
  • No test of cure for C. difficile. The nucleic acid test can stay positive for weeks after successful treatment. Judge response clinically.
  • No stool cultures after about three days of hospitalization. Diarrhea beginning well into an admission is far more likely from medications, tube feeds, or C. difficile than from a community enteric pathogen, and routine stool cultures in that setting have a very low yield.
  • No surface swabs of chronic wounds or ulcers. They report colonizing flora and drive broad, pointless therapy. If infection is genuinely suspected, obtain deep tissue after debridement, or bone for suspected osteomyelitis.
  • No repeat cultures just because the first was positive. Follow-up blood cultures are genuinely indicated in defined situations — S. aureus bacteremia, candidemia, endovascular infection — and are not a routine reflex elsewhere.
  • No panel because the panel exists. Ask in advance: what will I do differently if this comes back positive? If there is no answer, the result will only create work and risk.
What to do next

Before you sign any microbiology order, say the sentence out loud: “I am asking whether this patient has this infection in this compartment, and if the answer is yes I will do this.” Orders that survive that sentence are usually good ones. Orders that do not are the origin of most of the confusing results you will be asked to explain.

Reading a micro report as it evolves

A microbiology report is not a single answer that appears at a moment in time. It is a document that grows over several days, and each version answers a different question.

Hour 0–1Direct stain: “Gram-positive cocci in clusters” — class of organism, no species
Day 1Preliminary: growth present, identification in progress
Day 1–2Identification: “Staphylococcus aureus isolated. Susceptibilities to follow.”
Day 2–3Susceptibilities: MICs with S/I/R interpretations — the cue to narrow therapy
Day 5Final: “No growth, 5 days” or the completed isolate report
Clinical pattern

“Normal respiratory flora,” “mixed flora,” or “normal skin flora” is a real result, not a failed one. It means the laboratory found the organisms expected at that body site and no recognized pathogen in a meaningful amount. In a patient who is not improving, that is a genuine signal — look for a different site, a different organism class that this culture cannot detect, a noninfectious cause, or a specimen that never reached the infected compartment.

Student pearl

When you present a micro result on rounds, always state four things: the specimen source, the collection date, whether the result is preliminary or final, and whether the patient was on antibiotics when it was drawn. Those four qualifiers change the interpretation more than the organism name does.

Common questions

Why can’t the lab just tell me the susceptibility on day one?

Because conventional susceptibility testing measures whether a standardized number of living organisms grows in the presence of a drug, and you cannot standardize an inoculum you do not yet have. The organism has to grow first. Rapid molecular panels partly bridge the gap by detecting selected resistance genes off a positive blood culture bottle, but they only look for the specific markers on the panel — the absence of a resistance gene is not the same as a susceptible MIC, and phenotypic testing still follows.

The Gram stain said Gram-negative rods but the culture grew something else. Was the stain wrong?

Usually not. A stain samples one drop and reports morphology only, while culture samples the whole specimen over days and can recover organisms that were present in smaller numbers or that grow faster. Polymicrobial specimens are common, over-decolorization can make a Gram-positive organism look Gram-negative, and some organisms stain unreliably. The two tests are complementary; where they conflict, the culture generally wins for identification, and the stain remains the better evidence of how much inflammation was present.

If a swab is so bad, why does the hospital stock so many of them?

Because swabs are cheap, painless, and sometimes exactly right — a nasopharyngeal swab for a respiratory viral panel, a throat swab for group A streptococcus, a genital swab for gonorrhea and chlamydia nucleic acid testing. The problem is using a swab as a substitute for sampling a deep compartment. Swabs are appropriate for mucosal surface targets where you already know which organism you are asking about; they are a poor choice for abscesses, deep tissue, and anaerobic infection.

The patient already got a dose of antibiotics. Is it still worth sending cultures?

Yes, in most cases — but say so on the requisition and interpret accordingly. Yield falls, sometimes sharply, and it falls fastest for blood cultures. Some specimens remain informative anyway: tissue and abscess material often still grow, molecular tests do not require living organisms, and antigen tests are largely unaffected. What you should not do is delay the antibiotic in an unstable patient in order to obtain a cleaner culture. Draw what you can, treat, and document the timing so that a later negative result is read as “partially treated,” not as “no infection.”

What actually counts as a “sterile site”?

Blood, cerebrospinal fluid, joint fluid, pleural, pericardial and peritoneal fluid, deep tissue, and bone are normally free of organisms. Skin, the mouth and upper airway, the distal urethra, the vagina, and the entire gastrointestinal tract are not. This distinction is the reason the same organism can be a medical emergency in one specimen and an expected finding in another, and it is the single most useful filter to apply before you interpret any culture report.

Laboratory protocols, panel menus, rejection criteria, and incubation times differ meaningfully between institutions. When a result does not make sense, calling the laboratory and asking how that specimen was actually processed is a normal, expected, and usually very fast way to resolve it.

Pillar 02 · Reading the organisms

From a Gram stain to a shortlist.

You do not need to memorize a taxonomy. You need to look at a Gram stain report — purple or pink, round or rod-shaped, clusters or chains — and produce a short, clinically useful list of what this could be and what it would mean.

A Gram stain is the fastest meaningful information the microbiology lab will ever give you. It arrives in minutes to hours, long before a final identification, and it is almost always enough to narrow a universe of organisms down to a shortlist of five or six. This pillar teaches you to read that shortlist — and, more importantly, to know what each name on it means for the patient in front of you.

What a Gram stain actually tells you

The Gram stain is a four-step dye procedure that sorts bacteria by cell wall architecture. Organisms with a thick peptidoglycan layer trap the initial purple dye and stay purple-blue: Gram-positive. Organisms with a thin peptidoglycan layer and an outer membrane wash clear during the decolorizing step and pick up the pink counterstain: Gram-negative. That single distinction — plus shape and arrangement — is the organizing spine of clinical bacteriology.

  • Color tells you cell wall type: purple-blue is Gram-positive, pink-red is Gram-negative.
  • Shape tells you cocci (round) versus bacilli, usually called rods.
  • Arrangement is the most underused clue: clusters versus chains versus pairs versus single cells.
  • The host response matters too. The lab reports white blood cells on the smear. Abundant neutrophils with no organisms is itself a finding.
  • Specimen quality is graded on the same smear. Many squamous epithelial cells in a sputum sample means you collected saliva, not lower airway secretions.
Why it matters

Empiric antibiotics are usually started before any organism is named. A Gram stain read at 2 a.m. that says “Gram-positive cocci in clusters in both blood culture bottles” changes management immediately — you are now treating for Staphylococcus aureus until proven otherwise, which means covering methicillin-resistant strains and starting to think about echocardiography and source control. Waiting for the final identification costs you a day.

Pitfall

A Gram stain needs a substantial organism burden to be visible — on the order of tens of thousands of organisms per milliliter. Low-burden infections, partially treated infections, and organisms that stain poorly or not at all will produce a blank smear in a genuinely infected patient. “No organisms seen” is not a negative result.

The Gram stain decision tree

1
Are organisms seen at all?

Before morphology, ask whether anything is there. The answer shapes how much weight you put on the rest of the smear.

Organisms seen → proceed

Note the color, the shape, and the arrangement, and note whether the organisms sit inside neutrophils. Intracellular organisms argue strongly for true infection rather than surface contamination.

None seen → do not relax

Consider prior antibiotics, low organism burden, an organism that does not Gram stain (Mycoplasma, Legionella, Chlamydia, mycobacteria), a non-bacterial cause, or the wrong specimen. Keep treating the patient, not the smear.

2
What color are they?

This is the cell wall question, and it maps directly onto which antibiotic classes can even reach their target.

Purple-blue → Gram-positive

Thick peptidoglycan, no outer membrane. Go to step 3.

Pink-red → Gram-negative

Thin peptidoglycan with an outer membrane that acts as a permeability barrier. Go to step 5.

3
Gram-positive: cocci or rods?

Round or elongated. This is a bigger clinical fork than it looks.

Cocci → step 4

The large and clinically dominant group: staphylococci, streptococci, enterococci.

Rods → think in three buckets

A true pathogen at the extremes of age or in immunosuppression (Listeria); skin or environmental flora that is usually a contaminant (Corynebacterium and Cutibacterium from skin, Bacillus from the environment) but real on hardware; or an anaerobe (Clostridium species).

4
Gram-positive cocci: how are they arranged?

Arrangement follows the plane in which the organism divides, and it separates two completely different clinical worlds.

Clusters → Staphylococcus

Grape-like clumps. Next question is coagulase: positive means S. aureus, negative means coagulase-negative staphylococci.

Chains or pairs → Streptococcus / Enterococcus

Next question is hemolysis on a blood agar plate: beta (complete clearing), alpha (green partial), or gamma (none).

5
Gram-negative: rods or diplococci?

Shape is decisive here — the two groups share almost no clinical overlap.

Rods → gut versus water

Split into Enterobacterales, which come from the gastrointestinal tract, and non-fermenters such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which come from moist environmental reservoirs and are far more drug-resistant.

Diplococci → Neisseria (or Moraxella)

Kidney-bean-shaped pairs. In cerebrospinal fluid or blood this is N. meningitidis until proven otherwise and is a same-hour emergency. In a respiratory specimen, consider Moraxella catarrhalis.

6
Not bacteria at all?

Large ovoid forms with budding, sometimes with elongated pseudohyphae, are yeast — most often Candida. Yeast stains Gram-positive but is several times the size of a coccus, which is how you tell them apart at the scope.

Gram-positive cocci: clusters versus chains

Clusters: Staphylococcus

Then ask: coagulase-positive or negative?

Staphylococci grow in grape-like clusters. The lab splits them with the coagulase test, and that one result carries most of the clinical weight.

Coagulase-positiveS. aureus
Coagulase-negativeCoNS, e.g. S. epidermidis

Staphylococcus aureus is a true pathogen essentially wherever you find it: skin and soft tissue infection, bacteremia, infective endocarditis, osteomyelitis and septic arthritis, and prosthetic device infection. It seeds distant sites from the bloodstream, so S. aureus bacteremia is never treated as a simple bloodstream event — it triggers a search for a source, repeat blood cultures to document clearance, echocardiography, and a prolonged intravenous course. Most centers involve infectious diseases consultation automatically.

Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) are the dominant flora of human skin, which makes them the single most common blood culture contaminant. But they are genuine pathogens on foreign material: prosthetic joints and valves, central venous catheters, pacemaker leads, and cerebrospinal fluid shunts, where they form biofilm.

Clinical pattern

The context does the interpreting. CoNS in one of four blood culture bottles from a well patient with no hardware is almost always contamination. The same organism in multiple sets from separate draws in a patient with a prosthetic valve is an infection until proven otherwise.

Advanced pearl

Staphylococcus lugdunensis is the exception that ruins the rule. It is technically coagulase-negative, but clinically it behaves like S. aureus — aggressive native valve endocarditis with valve destruction, deep abscess, and prosthetic joint infection. When a lab speciates a CoNS as S. lugdunensis, that is a deliberate signal to you, not a courtesy. Treat it seriously and image the valves.

Chains and pairs: Streptococcus and Enterococcus

Then ask: what does it do to blood agar?

These organisms divide along a single axis, so they line up in chains or sit in pairs. The next branch point is hemolysis — what the colony does to the red cells in a blood agar plate.

Beta-hemolyticComplete clearing, a transparent halo
Alpha-hemolyticPartial, green discoloration
Gamma / non-hemolyticNo change to the agar

Beta-hemolytic streptococci are then assigned a Lancefield group letter — group A and group B are the two you must know. Alpha-hemolytic organisms split into Streptococcus pneumoniae and the viridans group. Non-hemolytic Gram-positive cocci in chains from a gut or urinary source should make you think Enterococcus.

Student pearl

S. pneumoniae has a distinctive look on the smear: lancet-shaped (pointed-oval) Gram-positive diplococci, often with a clear halo from the polysaccharide capsule. In a patient with pneumonia, meningitis, or otitis media, that morphology is close to diagnostic and should prompt you to think about the capsule — which is why asplenic and sickle cell patients are at such high risk and why pneumococcal vaccination matters.

Why it matters

Enterococcus is intrinsically resistant to all cephalosporins. If your patient is on ceftriaxone for a presumed intra-abdominal or urinary source and is not improving, enterococcal infection is one of the first gaps to consider. This is a property of the organism, not acquired resistance, so no susceptibility test will rescue a cephalosporin here.

Do not confuse

S. aureus and coagulase-negative staph are both “Gram-positive cocci in clusters” on the preliminary report — and they are not the same problem. One is a pathogen that seeds heart valves and bone and demands weeks of intravenous therapy; the other is usually skin flora that fell into the bottle. You will often see the cluster morphology hours before the coagulase result. In that interval, treat empirically for S. aureus in a sick patient, but do not commit a stable patient with a single positive bottle to a long course before the speciation returns. Ask the lab how many bottles and how many separate sets are positive — that number is frequently more informative than the organism name.

Gram-positive rods

This is the group students most often wave away as contamination. Sometimes that is right. The cost of being wrong is concentrated in a small number of scenarios, so learn those.

Listeria monocytogenes

The one you cannot afford to miss

A Gram-positive rod that causes bacteremia and meningoencephalitis, with a strong preference for four groups: neonates, pregnant patients, adults over roughly 50 to 65 years, and people with impaired cell-mediated immunity (corticosteroids, transplant, certain biologics, hematologic malignancy). Classically acquired from refrigerated ready-to-eat foods — deli meats, soft cheeses, unpasteurized dairy.

Key resistanceIntrinsically resistant to all cephalosporins
Why it matters

This is precisely why ampicillin is added to empiric bacterial meningitis coverage at the extremes of age and in immunocompromised patients. Ceftriaxone plus vancomycin — the standard adult meningitis backbone — has no meaningful activity against Listeria. If you are covering a 74-year-old for meningitis and have not added ampicillin, you have left a hole.

Corynebacterium and Bacillus

Usually contaminants, occasionally not

Corynebacterium species, often reported descriptively as “diphtheroids,” are normal skin flora and are among the most common blood culture contaminants. They become real on prosthetic material and in profoundly immunocompromised or heavily line-dependent patients. C. diphtheriae is a separate, toxin-mediated disease, now rare where vaccination coverage is high.

Bacillus species are environmental spore-formers and likewise frequent contaminants. Two matter: B. cereus causes toxin-mediated food poisoning (classically reheated rice) and, less commonly, serious catheter-associated and ocular infection; B. anthracis causes anthrax and is a recognized biothreat agent.

What to do next

If your lab flags a possible B. anthracis, do not handle it further on the open bench. Suspected anthrax triggers a defined laboratory and public health notification pathway. In practice, your job is to tell the lab the clinical story that raised the suspicion.

Anaerobic Gram-positive rods

Slow, indolent, and easy to miss

Cutibacterium acnes (formerly Propionibacterium acnes) lives in sebaceous follicles and is the classic indolent pathogen of shoulder arthroplasty and neurosurgical shunts and hardware. It grows slowly, so if you suspect it you must tell the lab to hold cultures for an extended incubation — often up to two weeks. A standard five-day culture will simply be reported as negative.

Clostridium species are anaerobic spore-formers: C. perfringens in gas gangrene and myonecrosis, C. tetani, C. botulinum. Clostridioides difficile was reclassified out of the genus Clostridium — which is why the name changed — but is still an anaerobic spore-former; it is diagnosed on stool by toxin and molecular testing, not by culture — do not order a stool culture expecting to find it.

Advanced pearl

Clostridium septicum bacteremia carries a well-recognized association with colonic malignancy and with neutropenic enterocolitis. Finding it in blood is an indication to evaluate the colon once the patient is stable.

Do not confuse

“Gram-positive rods, probably a contaminant” is a reasonable reflex in a blood culture and a dangerous one in cerebrospinal fluid. A Gram-positive rod in CSF is Listeria until proven otherwise, and the patient needs ampicillin now. The same logic applies to joint fluid from a prosthetic shoulder, where a “skin organism” is often the actual pathogen. Site changes the prior probability more than morphology does.

Gram-negative rods: gut origin versus water origin

Enterobacterales

From the gastrointestinal tract

The large family of Gram-negative rods that normally live in the gut and cause disease when they reach somewhere they do not belong: the urinary tract, the peritoneum, the biliary tree, the bloodstream.

Core membersE. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Proteus, Serratia, Citrobacter
Typical syndromesUTI and pyelonephritis, intra-abdominal infection, cholangitis, bacteremia. This family also contains the enteric pathogens — Salmonella, Shigella, and Shiga toxin–producing E. coli. Antibiotics are avoided in suspected Shiga toxin–producing E. coli because they increase the risk of hemolytic uremic syndrome.

Escherichia coli is the most common cause of uncomplicated urinary tract infection and a leading cause of Gram-negative bloodstream infection. Klebsiella pneumoniae causes pneumonia, liver abscess, and urinary infection. Proteus mirabilis produces urease, which alkalinizes urine and drives struvite stone formation — worth remembering in recurrent stone formers and chronically catheterized patients.

Advanced pearl

Several members — Enterobacter cloacae complex, Citrobacter freundii, Serratia marcescens, and relatives — carry an inducible chromosomal AmpC beta-lactamase. They can test susceptible to third-generation cephalosporins on day one and become resistant during therapy. Many institutions therefore avoid ceftriaxone for serious infections with these organisms even when the report says susceptible. Local practice varies; check your antimicrobial stewardship guidance.

Non-fermenters

From moist environments and the hospital

These Gram-negative rods do not ferment glucose, live in water and moist surfaces rather than the gut, and are substantially harder to treat.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the one to know. It is oxidase-positive — the classic quick test that separates it from Enterobacterales — and often produces a green pigment and a grape-like odor. It causes ventilator-associated pneumonia, infection in neutropenic and burn patients, otitis externa, and chronic airway infection in cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis. It is intrinsically resistant to many agents, including ceftriaxone and most oral antibiotics, so “broad-spectrum” does not automatically mean “covers Pseudomonas.”

Acinetobacter baumanniiICU, ventilated and wounded patients; often extensively resistant
Stenotrophomonas maltophiliaEmerges after broad-spectrum therapy; intrinsically carbapenem-resistant
Clinical pattern

Non-fermenters cluster in a recognizable patient: prolonged hospitalization, indwelling devices, mechanical ventilation, prior broad-spectrum antibiotics, neutropenia, or structural lung disease. A healthy outpatient with a first urinary tract infection is very unlikely to have one.

Advanced pearl

Acinetobacter is a short, plump coccobacillus that decolorizes incompletely and can be misread as a Gram-positive organism on a smear. If the morphology looks odd and the patient is a long-stay ICU patient, wait for the identification before anchoring.

Fastidious Gram-negatives and the Gram-negative diplococci

Some Gram-negatives are small, faint, and hard to grow, which historically made them look like “culture-negative” infections. Modern automated blood culture systems handle most of them.

  • Haemophilus influenzae — tiny pleomorphic coccobacilli that will not grow on ordinary blood agar because they need two growth factors supplied by chocolate agar. Non-typeable strains cause otitis media, sinusitis, and exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; invasive type b disease is now uncommon where the Hib vaccine is used.
  • Moraxella catarrhalis — note that this is a Gram-negative diplococcus, not a rod. Respiratory pathogen in otitis, sinusitis, and COPD exacerbation; nearly all strains produce beta-lactamase.
  • HACEK group — Haemophilus species, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, and Kingella. Oropharyngeal flora that cause subacute endocarditis. Once considered a cause of culture-negative endocarditis; with current systems they usually grow within the standard incubation period. Eikenella corrodens is a classic human bite wound organism, and Kingella kingae is a common cause of septic arthritis and osteomyelitis in young children.
  • Neisseria meningitidis — Gram-negative diplococci in CSF or blood. Meningitis with rapid deterioration, petechial or purpuric rash, and in severe cases purpura fulminans. Recurrent disease should prompt evaluation for terminal complement deficiency. Close contacts need chemoprophylaxis, and the case is reportable to public health.
  • Neisseria gonorrhoeae — in practice diagnosed by nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT), not culture. Culture is still used when susceptibility data are needed, such as suspected treatment failure, and requires selective media and prompt transport. A Gram stain of urethral discharge in a symptomatic man showing intracellular Gram-negative diplococci is useful; the same stain from cervical, rectal, or pharyngeal sites is not reliable.

What to do next

Gram-negative diplococci in CSF is a same-hour event. Give antibiotics immediately if they are not already running, place the patient in droplet precautions, notify infection prevention and public health, and start identifying close contacts for chemoprophylaxis. Do not wait for the culture to finalize before doing any of these.

Anaerobes

When to suspect an anaerobe

The clinical picture beats the culture

Anaerobes are slow-growing, fussy about transport, and frequently not recovered even when present. You will usually make this call clinically.

  • Foul-smelling discharge or drainage — a genuinely useful bedside sign
  • An abscess or any walled-off collection of pus
  • Gas in soft tissue on examination or imaging
  • Tissue necrosis, devitalized tissue, or impaired blood supply
  • Infections below the diaphragm — intra-abdominal, pelvic, perirectal
  • Aspiration pneumonia, lung abscess, and dental or periodontal infection
  • Human and animal bite wounds
Pitfall

A dry swab rubbed across the surface of a wound is close to useless for anaerobic culture — the organisms die on the way to the lab and you sample skin flora rather than the infection. Send aspirated pus in a syringe or, better, a tissue sample, in an anaerobic transport container. Talk to the lab before you collect if you are unsure.

The Bacteroides fragilis group dominates colonic flora, produces beta-lactamases, and is the reason intra-abdominal regimens include specific anaerobic coverage. Peptostreptococcus and related anaerobic Gram-positive cocci turn up in mixed abscesses almost anywhere.

A practical point: most laboratories do not routinely perform susceptibility testing on anaerobes. Anaerobic infections are therefore usually treated empirically based on site and known resistance patterns, with testing reserved for unusual or refractory cases. This is standard and not a gap in your workup.

Advanced pearl

Fusobacterium necrophorum causes Lemierre syndrome: a young, previously healthy patient with recent pharyngitis develops septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein and showers septic emboli to the lungs. The tip-off is persistent fever and neck pain after a sore tooth or sore throat that should have resolved, plus nodular lung lesions. It is uncommon, genuinely dangerous, and easy to attribute to something else.

Organisms that do not play by Gram-stain rules

For this group, ordering a routine bacterial culture and waiting is a way of generating a falsely reassuring negative. Each one needs a specific test, and the lab generally will not run it unless you ask.

The atypical respiratory pathogens

Invisible on a routine sputum Gram stain

Mycoplasma pneumoniae has no cell wall at all, which is the whole clinical point: beta-lactam antibiotics have no target and do not work. Diagnosis is by PCR on a respiratory specimen. Treatment relies on macrolides, tetracyclines, or respiratory fluoroquinolones.

Legionella pneumophila is an intracellular organism that stains poorly and will not grow on standard media — it requires special charcoal-based agar. In practice it is diagnosed by urinary antigen or PCR. The urinary antigen is convenient but detects only one serogroup, so a negative result does not exclude legionellosis.

Chlamydia trachomatis and Chlamydia pneumoniae are obligate intracellular organisms diagnosed by nucleic acid amplification testing.

Clinical pattern

A sputum Gram stain crowded with neutrophils but showing no organisms in a patient with a real pneumonia is a recognized signature of these pathogens — and also of partially treated infection. Do not read it as “no infection.”

Mycobacteria and Nocardia

Acid-fast, and slow

Mycobacteria have a waxy, lipid-rich cell wall that resists Gram staining. They are demonstrated with acid-fast stains, either the traditional carbolfuchsin method or fluorescent staining.

M. tuberculosisGrows over weeks; molecular tests give a much faster preliminary answer
Non-tuberculous mycobacteriaStructural lung disease, skin and soft tissue, hardware infection

Nocardia is a branching, filamentous, partially acid-fast Gram-positive organism seen in immunocompromised patients — often pulmonary disease with a tendency to spread to the brain. It also grows slowly.

What to do next

A routine bacterial culture will not find any of these. You must explicitly order acid-fast bacilli smear and culture, or Nocardia culture, and it helps enormously to tell the lab why. Prolonged incubation and special media are only used when requested.

Spirochetes and vector-borne organisms

Serology and PCR territory

Treponema pallidum, the cause of syphilis, cannot be grown in routine culture at all. Diagnosis is serologic, using a combination of treponemal and non-treponemal tests.

Rickettsia, Ehrlichia, and Anaplasma are obligate intracellular, tick-transmitted organisms diagnosed by PCR and serology. Serology in the acute phase is often still negative.

Bartonella causes cat scratch disease and is a recognized cause of blood-culture-negative endocarditis; diagnosis is by serology and molecular testing, sometimes on the excised valve.

Why it matters

For suspected Rocky Mountain spotted fever and related rickettsial illness, treatment with doxycycline should not wait for confirmatory testing. Delay is associated with worse outcomes, and the confirmatory serology frequently does not turn positive until convalescence. This is a situation where the correct move is to treat on clinical suspicion and let the laboratory catch up.

Fungi at a practical level

Candida

The yeast you will meet constantly

Candida is normal flora of the gut, mouth, and vagina, so it appears in specimens all the time. The entire clinical skill is separating colonization from infection.

C. albicansMost common; usually azole-susceptible
C. glabrataReduced azole susceptibility; species identification changes therapy
C. parapsilosisLine-associated; source control is central
C. aurisInfection control emergency

Candidemia is always significant. There is no such thing as a contaminant Candida blood culture. It requires antifungal therapy, a search for and removal of the source (central lines in particular), repeat blood cultures to document clearance, and — per Infectious Diseases Society of America guidance — a dilated ophthalmologic examination, since endophthalmitis is sight-threatening and often asymptomatic. The optimal timing of that exam is debated, and some centers have narrowed the indication; follow your local pathway.

Advanced pearl

Candida auris is a distinct problem for the laboratory as much as the patient. It is frequently multidrug-resistant, it colonizes skin and persists on environmental surfaces, it spreads within healthcare facilities, and older biochemical identification systems misidentify it as other yeasts. A confirmed or suspected isolate triggers immediate contact precautions and notification of infection prevention.

Molds

Neutropenia and transplantation

Aspergillus is the mold you must know. Invasive aspergillosis occurs in prolonged neutropenia, hematopoietic stem cell and solid organ transplant, and sustained high-dose corticosteroid therapy. It is angioinvasive, which produces the characteristic nodular and cavitary pulmonary lesions.

Supportive testingGalactomannan on serum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid
Interpretation cautionA respiratory culture in a non-immunocompromised patient often reflects colonization
Advanced pearl

Mucormycosis, caused by the Mucorales molds, is the other angioinvasive mold syndrome — classically in poorly controlled diabetes with ketoacidosis, or in iron overload. It presents as rhino-orbito-cerebral or pulmonary disease and progresses fast. It is important partly because voriconazole, a mainstay for Aspergillus, does not cover it. Rapid surgical debridement plus an active agent is the treatment, so the distinction is urgent rather than academic.

Cryptococcus and Pneumocystis

Immunosuppression, diagnosed without culture

Cryptococcus neoformans is an encapsulated yeast causing subacute meningitis, classically in advanced HIV with a low CD4 count but also in transplant recipients and patients on corticosteroids. The cryptococcal antigen test on serum and cerebrospinal fluid is sensitive and fast, and it is how the diagnosis is usually made. Management includes attention to raised intracranial pressure, which frequently requires repeated therapeutic lumbar punctures.

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) cannot be cultured at all. Diagnosis is by direct detection — immunofluorescent staining or PCR — on induced sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. The typical patient has advanced HIV, is on chronic corticosteroids or other immunosuppression, and presents with subacute dyspnea and hypoxemia that is out of proportion to the chest examination.

Student pearl

For both of these, the diagnostic test is something you have to think of and order by name. Neither will appear on a routine culture report, so a “negative workup” in an immunosuppressed patient means very little unless you check which tests were actually sent.

Do not confuse

Candida growing in a specimen is not the same as candidiasis. Candida in sputum or a tracheal aspirate essentially never represents Candida pneumonia in an adult, and treating it is one of the most common unnecessary antifungal exposures in the hospital. Candida from a superficial wound swab or from the urine of an asymptomatic catheterized patient is usually colonization as well; asymptomatic candiduria is generally not treated except in defined situations such as neutropenia or before a urologic procedure. Meanwhile Candida in blood, in a normally sterile fluid, or in intraoperative tissue is always real and always needs treatment. Same organism, opposite decisions — the site is what determines meaning.

Geography, parasites, and viruses — brief but not optional

Endemic dimorphic fungi are driven by where the patient has been, so the exposure history is the diagnostic test that matters most. Histoplasma is associated with the Ohio and Mississippi River valleys and with bird and bat exposure; Blastomyces overlaps that distribution and extends into the upper Midwest and Southeast; Coccidioides is found in the desert Southwest. Antigen testing and serology carry much of the diagnostic load.

Pitfall

If you suspect coccidioidomycosis, say so on the requisition. The mold form is a genuine laboratory-acquired infection hazard and needs to be handled under enhanced containment. This is one of the clearest examples of clinical information directly protecting laboratory staff.

Parasites are a small but high-stakes list for a rotation. The one that cannot be missed is malaria: any febrile patient returning from an endemic area needs urgent thick and thin blood smears, usually paired with a rapid antigen test. Plasmodium falciparum can progress to severe disease within hours, so this is a same-shift test, not a next-morning test. Also worth knowing: Giardia as a cause of prolonged watery diarrhea after freshwater or travel exposure, diagnosed by stool antigen; and Toxoplasma as a cause of brain lesions in advanced immunosuppression.

Viruses are now almost entirely a molecular discipline. Multiplex respiratory PCR panels identify influenza, respiratory syncytial virus, SARS-CoV-2, and others from a single swab. HIV is diagnosed with a combination antigen/antibody immunoassay, confirmed by an HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay; HIV-1 nucleic acid testing is reserved for discordant or indeterminate results, where it identifies acute infection. Hepatitis relies on serologic panels. In transplant and other immunosuppressed patients, cytomegalovirus and Epstein-Barr virus are followed with quantitative viral loads. Herpes simplex virus PCR on cerebrospinal fluid is the test for suspected viral encephalitis.

The available viral panels, their exact organism content, and their turnaround times differ substantially between institutions. Look up what your own laboratory offers rather than assuming.

Translating the Gram stain into a bedside decision

The question this answers: do I need coverage for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, and is this a contaminant?

  • Cocci in clusters, sick patient, any site. Assume S. aureus and cover for it, including resistant strains, until the coagulase result and susceptibilities return.
  • Cocci in clusters, well patient, one bottle, no hardware. Most likely coagulase-negative staph contamination. Repeat cultures and reassess rather than committing to therapy.
  • Cocci in chains or lancet-shaped pairs. Think Streptococcus. Beta-lactams generally work well; the exception is Enterococcus, which no cephalosporin covers.
  • Rods. Ask the site question first. In blood from a well patient, likely skin flora. In CSF, in a prosthetic joint, or in a shunt, take it seriously.
What to do next

For any Gram-positive cocci in clusters from blood, ask three things: how many bottles and how many separate sets are positive, does the patient have any implanted hardware, and have follow-up blood cultures been drawn. Those three answers usually settle the contaminant question before the lab does.

The question this answers: is this a gut organism I can treat conventionally, or a resistant environmental organism that needs antipseudomonal coverage?

  • Rods, community-acquired urinary or abdominal source. Most likely Enterobacterales. Standard Gram-negative coverage guided by local resistance patterns is usually appropriate.
  • Rods, prolonged hospitalization, ventilator, neutropenia, burns, or cystic fibrosis. Cover Pseudomonas until the identification returns. Remember that ceftriaxone does not.
  • Diplococci in CSF or blood. Treat as meningococcal disease now — antibiotics, droplet precautions, public health notification, contact prophylaxis.
  • Tiny faint coccobacilli in a respiratory specimen. Consider Haemophilus or Moraxella; beta-lactamase production is common in both.
Why it matters

The gut-versus-environment split is the single most useful Gram-negative distinction for empiric therapy, because it predicts intrinsic resistance. Getting it wrong in a neutropenic patient with Pseudomonas bacteremia costs hours that matter.

The question this answers: what am I failing to test for?

  • No cell wall: Mycoplasma. Beta-lactams are inert against it. Send respiratory PCR.
  • Intracellular or fastidious: Legionella (urinary antigen or PCR), Chlamydia (NAAT), Coxiella and Bartonella (serology, molecular).
  • Acid-fast: mycobacteria. Order acid-fast smear and culture explicitly and expect weeks, not days.
  • Not culturable: Treponema pallidum (serology) and Pneumocystis jirovecii (direct detection on a respiratory specimen).
  • Fungal: cryptococcal antigen, galactomannan, endemic fungal antigens — each is a separate order.
  • Viral: targeted or multiplex PCR, serology, or quantitative viral load depending on the organism.
Student pearl

The common thread is that none of these appear on a standard bacterial culture. When a clinical picture and a negative culture disagree, the right question is not “is there really an infection?” but “which test did nobody order?”

Questions students actually ask on this rotation

How quickly will the lab tell me a blood culture is positive, and what will they say?

Automated blood culture systems monitor bottles continuously and flag them as soon as growth is detected, so the call can come at any hour. The first communication is almost always the Gram stain — color, shape, arrangement, and which bottles are positive. Many laboratories now follow that immediately with a rapid multiplex molecular panel run directly off the positive bottle, which can name common organisms and detect selected resistance genes within about an hour. Definitive identification and full susceptibility testing follow over the next day or so. Availability of the rapid panels and exactly which targets they include varies considerably between institutions.

How does the lab actually identify the organism once it grows?

Most clinical laboratories now use matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS), which reads the protein fingerprint of a colony and matches it against a reference database in minutes. It replaced most of the biochemical test panels you may read about in older textbooks. Classic phenotypic tests — catalase, coagulase, oxidase, hemolysis on blood agar — are still used as fast bench-side triage and are still worth knowing, because they map directly onto the clinical reasoning in this pillar. Some organisms, including certain molds and unusual mycobacteria, still require molecular sequencing.

My sputum specimen was rejected. Why?

Because it was mostly saliva. Laboratories screen expectorated sputum by Gram stain and look at the ratio of squamous epithelial cells, which come from the mouth, to neutrophils. A specimen loaded with squamous cells — commonly more than about ten per low-power field, though thresholds vary by laboratory — is reporting on oral flora rather than the lower airway, and culturing it produces misleading results. The fix is a properly collected deep specimen, or a lower respiratory sample such as tracheal aspirate or bronchoalveolar lavage in the right patient. This rejection is the lab protecting you from a wrong answer, not being obstructive.

The report says “mixed flora” or “normal respiratory flora.” What does that mean?

It means the specimen grew the mixture of organisms expected from that body site and no single pathogen predominated. In urine, mixed growth usually indicates a collection problem — perineal or skin contamination — rather than polymicrobial infection, and the appropriate response is a properly collected repeat specimen if infection is still suspected. In a superficial wound swab, it usually means you sampled colonizing skin flora rather than infected tissue. Reporting and identifying every organism in these mixtures would generate noise, not information, so laboratories deliberately do not.

Does it help to tell the lab what I am worried about?

Substantially, and this is one of the most practical habits to build. Clinical information changes what the laboratory does: whether cultures are held for prolonged incubation for slow growers like Cutibacterium or HACEK organisms, whether selective or enriched media are set up, whether acid-fast or fungal stains and cultures are performed at all, and whether the specimen is handled under enhanced biosafety containment for organisms such as Coccidioides, Brucella, or Francisella. A requisition that says only “culture” buys you a standard workup. If you are chasing something unusual, call the laboratory — they will tell you what to send and how.

Why do some cultures get held for days longer than others?

Because organisms grow at very different rates and the incubation period is set to the target. Routine bacterial cultures are typically held for a standard short period because common pathogens grow quickly. Suspected prosthetic joint infection, suspected Cutibacterium, and some endocarditis workups are held longer, often up to two weeks, to catch indolent organisms. Fungal cultures may run for weeks, and mycobacterial cultures longer still. Extended holds usually require a specific request, so the culture that was “negative at five days” may simply not have been given the chance to grow the organism you are worried about. Exact hold protocols vary by laboratory.

How do I tell a blood culture contaminant from a real bloodstream infection?

No single feature is decisive, so weigh several together. Organisms that are essentially always pathogens when recovered from blood include S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, Enterobacterales, Pseudomonas, and Candida. Organisms that are frequently contaminants include coagulase-negative staphylococci, Corynebacterium, Bacillus, and Cutibacterium. Beyond identity, look at the proportion of bottles and sets that turned positive, whether the sets were drawn from separate sites at separate times, how quickly the bottles flagged, whether the patient has intravascular hardware, and whether the clinical picture actually fits bacteremia. Two properly collected sets before antibiotics is what makes this interpretation possible — one set gives you almost no ability to discriminate.

Practical note: where this pillar describes what a laboratory does — incubation periods, rapid panel availability, specimen rejection thresholds, extended-hold protocols, and which identification platforms are in use — expect real variation between institutions. The clinical reasoning transfers everywhere; the local operational details do not. Find out how your own laboratory works.

Pillar 03 · Susceptibility, resistance & stewardship

The report says S. That is necessary, not sufficient.

This is where a half-understanding is genuinely dangerous. What a minimum inhibitory concentration is (and why you must never compare it between drugs), what susceptible really predicts, the resistance patterns worth knowing by name, and how to narrow therapy without losing the patient.

A susceptibility report is not a ranking of antibiotics from best to worst. It is a set of predictions — organism by organism, drug by drug — about whether a standard dose is likely to work at the usual site of infection. Learning to read it correctly, and knowing where it stops being trustworthy, is one of the highest-yield things you can take away from a day in the microbiology laboratory.

What every line of the report is made of

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is the lowest concentration of an antibiotic that prevents visible growth of that isolate in vitro, under standardized conditions of medium, inoculum, temperature and incubation time. It is usually reported in micrograms per milliliter, and because the lab tests doubling dilutions, MICs come in a familiar ladder: 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32.

Two things follow from that definition and are worth holding onto. First, the MIC is a property of this isolate against this drug — it is a laboratory measurement, not a clinical outcome. Second, values reported as ≤ or ≥ a number (for example ≤ 1 or ≥ 32) mean the true MIC fell off the end of the tested range, so the panel simply reports the edge.

Student pearl

A doubling-dilution ladder means a one-step difference in MIC is within the normal reproducibility of the method. Do not read meaning into a change from 1 to 2 on the same drug for the same bug.

The MIC is only interpretable once it is compared with that drug’s breakpoint for that organism — a threshold set by standards bodies such as CLSI (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute) in much of the United States and EUCAST (European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing) elsewhere, taking into account achievable drug concentrations, the dosing regimen, how the organism’s MICs are normally distributed, and clinical outcome data.

S — susceptibleLikely to respond to standard dosing at the usual site of infection.
I — intermediateA buffer zone. May respond where the drug is concentrated (classically urine) or with higher dosing. For certain organism–drug pairs this category has been redefined as susceptible-dose dependent (SDD), meaning the drug can work but only if the higher approved dose is actually used.
R — resistantDo not expect a response at achievable concentrations. Choose something else.

Every one of these is a prediction, not a measurement of what will happen in your patient. And breakpoints are revised — as pharmacokinetic understanding and outcome data accumulate, thresholds are periodically lowered, so an MIC that was called susceptible some years ago may be called intermediate or resistant today.

Advanced pearl

CLSI and EUCAST do not always agree, and automated instruments need software and panel updates before a revised breakpoint reaches the printed report. That is why two hospitals can report different categories for genuinely identical MICs, and why the microbiology laboratory director is the right person to ask which criteria your report is using.

Four methods, all trying to answer the same question.

  • Broth microdilution — the reference method. The organism is grown in a tray of wells containing doubling dilutions of each drug; the first clear well is the MIC.
  • Automated systems — miniaturized dilution panels read by an instrument. This is what most hospital labs run for routine isolates, and it is why panels are fixed menus rather than made to order.
  • Disk diffusion — paper disks on an agar lawn; the diameter of the clear zone of inhibition is measured in millimeters and interpreted against zone breakpoints. No MIC is produced.
  • Gradient diffusion strips — a plastic strip carrying a concentration gradient; the MIC is read where the ellipse of growth meets the strip. Useful for single drugs not on the automated panel.

Practically: susceptibilities generally take about another day after the organism has grown, because the lab needs a pure, standardized inoculum before it can start. Fastidious and slow-growing organisms take longer, and some (many anaerobes, most Mycobacteria) are only tested at reference laboratories or on request.

Why it matters

If you know that “final susceptibilities tomorrow” is normal, you will stop waiting for perfect data and start planning the empiric-to-targeted transition on day one — which is where stewardship actually happens.

Do not confuse

You cannot compare MICs between different antibiotics to decide which drug is stronger. An MIC of 0.5 for drug A and 8 for drug B tells you nothing about which agent to pick, because each drug has its own breakpoint derived from its own achievable concentrations. The only legitimate comparisons are MIC versus that same drug’s breakpoint, and the same drug across isolates of the same species. Comparing across drugs is the single most common — and most dangerous — misreading of a susceptibility report.

Worked example: an Escherichia coli from a urine culture

Ampicillin≥ 32 · R Ceftriaxone≤ 1 · S Ciprofloxacin0.5 · I Nitrofurantoin32 · S

Read the categories, not the numbers. Ciprofloxacin has the lower MIC of the two systemic options here and is the worse choice — it sits in the intermediate range for its own breakpoint, while ceftriaxone at a numerically higher MIC is comfortably susceptible. Nitrofurantoin, at an MIC of 32 — far higher than either systemic option — is fully susceptible, because its breakpoint reflects the very high concentrations achieved in urine and nowhere else.

Clinical pattern

Whenever a drug is reported susceptible at an MIC that looks implausibly high, ask where that drug concentrates. Almost always the answer is urine — and almost always it means the agent belongs only in simple cystitis.

This is a designed nudge toward narrow prescribing, agreed locally between the laboratory, the antimicrobial stewardship program and pharmacy.

The antibiogram

What to do before your patient’s results exist

An antibiogram is your institution’s periodic — usually annual — summary of the percentage of isolates of each organism that tested susceptible to each drug. It answers the empiric question: for a patient with this syndrome at this hospital, what is the probability that a given agent covers the likely organism?

Reads asE. coli, ceftriaxone: 88% S

Standard construction rules (CLSI publishes guidance on this) include counting only the first isolate per patient per period and suppressing organisms with too few isolates to be statistically meaningful.

Pitfall

Applying the hospital-wide antibiogram to an ICU patient with prior antibiotic exposure. Aggregate numbers are dominated by outpatient and ward isolates and will systematically overestimate how well narrow agents cover a critically ill, previously treated patient.

Resistance you can predict without testing

Some organism–drug combinations never work, as a property of the species rather than of the isolate. This is intrinsic resistance, and the lab will often not even test the drug — because the answer is already known, and printing it would only invite someone to prescribe it.

  • Enterococcus and cephalosporins. No cephalosporin treats enterococci, at any dose. If your patient has enterococcal bacteremia on ceftriaxone, they are effectively untreated.
  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa and most simple agents. Ceftriaxone, ampicillin, and ertapenem do not cover it. Anti-pseudomonal coverage is a deliberate choice, not a byproduct of picking a broad-sounding drug.
  • Mycoplasma, Ureaplasma and all beta-lactams. These organisms have no cell wall, so a drug whose entire mechanism is cell-wall synthesis inhibition has no target.
  • Listeria monocytogenes and cephalosporins. This is precisely why ampicillin is added to empiric meningitis regimens for neonates, older adults and immunocompromised hosts — the cephalosporin does not cover Listeria.
  • Stenotrophomonas maltophilia and carbapenems. Intrinsically resistant; a patient deteriorating on meropenem with this organism is a classic scenario.
  • Anaerobes — variable. The Bacteroides fragilis group commonly produces beta-lactamase, so anaerobic coverage must be chosen intentionally. Routine anaerobic susceptibility testing is not performed at most hospitals; therapy is usually guided by published surveillance patterns.
Advanced pearl

Enterococcus often tests susceptible to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole in vitro yet fails in vivo, because the organism scavenges preformed folate available in tissue that is absent from the test medium. It is a clean example of the general principle that the test tube is a model of the patient, not the patient.

Supplementary tests worth knowing by name

The D-test — inducible clindamycin resistance

When a staphylococcal isolate tests erythromycin-resistant but clindamycin-susceptible, the lab performs a double-disk test: the two disks are placed near each other on the agar, and if erythromycin induces the resistance gene, the clindamycin zone flattens on the facing side into a D shape.

A positive D-test means clindamycin can fail during therapy as resistant subpopulations are selected, even though the isolate looked susceptible. The lab will normally report clindamycin as resistant in that situation. The same phenomenon is tested in beta-hemolytic streptococci.

Why it matters

Clindamycin is a common oral choice for skin and soft tissue infection and for bone and joint infection where prolonged therapy is planned. This is exactly the setting where an inducible failure would be discovered late.

Beta-lactamase testing

A rapid, direct test — most commonly a chromogenic cephalosporin that changes color when hydrolyzed — for organisms in which a single enzyme explains beta-lactam failure. It is used for organisms such as Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae and staphylococci, and gives an answer in minutes rather than a day.

A positive result predicts failure of penicillin or ampicillin. A negative result does not exclude every mechanism of resistance, so it is used alongside, not instead of, standard testing.

MRSA nares screening — a test used for its negative result

A nasal swab, usually tested by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), for MRSA colonization. Its value in pneumonia is almost entirely in the negative result: a patient who is not colonized is very unlikely to have MRSA pneumonia, so the swab has a high negative predictive value and supports stopping empiric anti-MRSA therapy.

Important limits. A positive swab means colonization, not infection, and is a poor reason to start or continue vancomycin on its own. The high negative predictive value is best established for pneumonia and should not be extrapolated to bacteremia, skin and soft tissue infection, or bone and joint infection. Its performance also depends on local prevalence and on how recently the swab was collected.

What to do next

On a patient started empirically on vancomycin for possible MRSA pneumonia, check whether a nares screen has been sent. A negative screen at 48 hours in an improving patient is one of the cleanest de-escalation opportunities on the ward.

Rapid molecular resistance markers from positive blood cultures

Many laboratories run a multiplex molecular panel directly on a blood culture bottle as soon as it flags positive. Alongside organism identification, these panels detect selected resistance genes — mecA or mecC for methicillin resistance in staphylococci, vanA and vanB for vancomycin resistance in enterococci, and carbapenemase genes such as KPC, NDM and OXA-48 in gram-negatives.

The result typically returns within about an hour of the bottle flagging, roughly a day earlier than conventional susceptibilities. That gap is where the clinical benefit lives: a Staphylococcus aureus in blood that is mecA-negative can move to a beta-lactam the same day, which is both narrower and, for methicillin-susceptible S. aureus bacteremia, the better-performing therapy.

Panels differ between institutions and detect only the targets on the menu — a gene that is not on the panel is not excluded by a negative result.

Susceptible on paper, wrong in the patient

Susceptibility is necessary but not sufficient. The test tube contains a fixed drug concentration and a pure organism in broth; your patient has tissue compartments, protein binding, surfactant, pus and hardware. These are the well-established places where an S can still be the wrong answer.

Pitfall

Treating the susceptibility report instead of the patient in front of you. “Susceptible” answers one question only: can this drug inhibit this organism at achievable concentrations. It says nothing about whether the drug reaches the site, whether the syndrome needs a procedure, whether the isolate is even the cause, or whether the patient needs an antibiotic at all.

Stewardship as a workflow

1
Culture before you treat, where it is safe to do so

Blood cultures and the relevant site specimen should be collected before the first dose whenever this does not delay therapy. In sepsis and septic shock, antibiotics are time-critical — draw cultures fast, but never postpone the drug. A single dose given first can render the culture sterile and cost you the chance to narrow later.

2
Choose empiric therapy from syndrome plus host plus local antibiogram

Three inputs, in that order. What is the likely syndrome and therefore the likely organisms? What about this host — immunosuppression, prior resistant organisms, recent antibiotics, recent hospitalization, devices — widens that list? And what does your unit’s antibiogram say about local susceptibility for those organisms? Then choose the narrowest agent that covers the likely pathogens with acceptable confidence.

3
Reassess at 48 to 72 hours — the antibiotic time-out

By now you usually have an organism, often susceptibilities, and always a clinical trajectory. Put a deliberate checkpoint in the notes. Three questions: is this still an infection, is this still the right drug, and how long will it run?

Organism identified and patient improving -> de-escalate

Switch to the narrowest agent the isolate is susceptible to that reaches the site of infection. Stop the redundant coverage — the empiric anti-MRSA agent, the second gram-negative drug, the antifungal added “just in case.” Narrowing is an active decision that has to be made; nothing narrows on its own.

Cultures negative or patient not improving -> stop and rethink

Do not simply broaden. Reconsider the diagnosis: is this infection at all, is the source controlled, is there an undrained focus, is the organism one the current drug never covered, is this a drug fever or a non-infectious mimic? Broadening without a hypothesis is how patients end up on four agents and no diagnosis.

4
Convert intravenous to oral once the patient qualifies

When the patient is clinically improving, hemodynamically stable, afebrile, able to take and absorb oral medication, and an oral agent with adequate bioavailability and site penetration exists — switch. This removes line risk, shortens stay and often enables discharge. Some syndromes and organisms require intravenous therapy regardless; check the specific situation.

5
Write a stop date the day you start

Every antibiotic order should carry an intended duration or a defined review date. “Continue antibiotics” with no endpoint is how a five-day course becomes three weeks. Shorter courses are increasingly the standard for many common infections, supported by randomized trials — community-acquired pneumonia and uncomplicated gram-negative bacteremia are the best-known examples. Confirm the duration for your specific syndrome against current guidance.

Where a medical student genuinely adds value

  • Being the person on the team who reads the final susceptibilities the morning they post, and proposes the narrower agent.
  • Asking on rounds, out loud: “what is the stop date?”
  • Taking a proper allergy history on the patient labeled penicillin-allergic, and flagging them for de-labeling.
  • Checking whether the MRSA nares screen came back negative on the patient still on vancomycin.
  • Asking whether the source is controlled before anyone proposes a broader drug.
Student pearl

De-escalation and duration are the two levers where a junior team member can change care safely and immediately. They require no prescribing authority — only that someone reads the data and asks the question.

Prescribing pitfalls that recur on every ward

Pitfall

Treating a positive culture rather than a patient. The archetype is asymptomatic bacteriuria: bacteria in the urine of a patient with no urinary symptoms. It is common in older adults, in residents of long-term care and in anyone with a urinary catheter, and treating it does not help — it selects resistance and predisposes to Clostridioides difficile. The recognized exceptions are narrow: pregnancy, and before urologic procedures expected to cause mucosal bleeding. A positive urine culture in a confused older patient with no urinary symptoms and another plausible explanation is not automatically a urinary tract infection.

Continuing broad empiric therapy after the organism is known

The most common stewardship failure in hospitals. Empiric therapy is a hedge against uncertainty; once the organism and susceptibilities are known, that uncertainty is gone and the hedge should come off. Broad coverage continued out of inertia adds toxicity, C. difficile risk and resistance pressure with no additional benefit.

Adding a drug instead of narrowing

When a patient is not improving, adding an agent feels like doing something. Usually the correct move is to stop and rethink: reconsider the diagnosis, look for an undrained source, check that the current drug reaches the site, and confirm the drug is actually being given. Escalation should follow a specific hypothesis about what is not covered — not general anxiety.

Accepting a penicillin allergy label at face value

A reported penicillin allergy is one of the most consequential entries in a chart: it pushes patients toward broader, more toxic and often less effective alternatives. Most reported penicillin allergies are not true immunoglobulin E–mediated allergy — many are childhood rashes, family history, or intolerances such as nausea, and true allergy can also wane over years.

Taking the history properly — what happened, how soon after the dose, how it was treated, and whether the patient has tolerated any beta-lactam since — often allows the label to be removed or narrowed. Cross-reactivity between penicillins and cephalosporins is substantially lower than historically taught and is driven largely by side-chain similarity rather than by the beta-lactam ring itself.

Why it matters

Allergy de-labeling is one of the few interventions that simultaneously narrows the spectrum, improves the efficacy of therapy and reduces cost. Many hospitals now run formal de-labeling pathways; ask whether yours does.

Forgetting to stop

Antibiotics started in the emergency department for a possible infection have a way of surviving every handover on the ward. If no one owns the indication, no one owns the stop date. Document the indication and intended duration in the note on day one, and revisit it at the 48-to-72-hour time-out.

Practice varies between institutions on several of the points above — which drugs are suppressed from reports, whether ESBL and AmpC phenotypes are flagged explicitly, which rapid molecular panels are available, and which agents are preferred for resistant gram-negative infection. Check your own laboratory’s reporting policy, your unit-specific antibiogram, and current national guidance rather than assuming the pattern you learned on one rotation is universal.

For each common infection: which specimen to send, what the laboratory will likely tell you, and the classic trap that catches teams every week. This pillar ties the first three back to the bedside.

Start at the bedside, not at the plate

Everything in the first three pillars — how specimens are collected, how organisms are identified, how susceptibility is measured — exists to answer one question: what should happen to this patient? The fastest way to become useful in the microbiology lab is to stop thinking organism-first and start thinking syndrome-first. You already know what sepsis, pneumonia, and meningitis look like. This pillar adds the four things you do not yet know for each one.

Four questions, every syndrome, every time

  • What specimen actually answers the question? Not the easiest specimen to get — the one that samples the infected compartment.
  • What is the lab likely to say? Knowing the expected flora tells you whether a result is signal or noise before you even see it.
  • How do I interpret it? The same organism can be a pathogen in one site and a bystander in another.
  • What is the classic pitfall? Nearly every syndrome has one recurring error that experienced clinicians watch for.
Why it matters

A culture result is not a diagnosis. It is one data point about a compartment of the body, collected at one moment, filtered through how it was obtained. The clinical question always outranks the plate.

1. Bloodstream infection and bacteremia

Blood is normally sterile, so anything that grows demands an explanation — either a true bloodstream infection or a break in collection technique. Your job is to make that distinction defensible.

Collect it properly

Volume and timing beat cleverness

Draw two sets from two separate venipuncture sites, before starting antibiotics whenever the patient's stability allows. A “set” is one aerobic plus one anaerobic bottle from a single draw.

Sets in adults2 sets, separate sites
Bottles per setaerobic + anaerobic
Biggest driver of yieldblood volume per bottle
Timingbefore antimicrobials

In adults, underfilled bottles are the most common reason a true bacteremia is missed. Follow your lab's stated fill volume — most institutions post it on the bottle or in the collection manual.

Student pearl

Two sets from two sites is not bureaucratic ritual. It is the entire mechanism by which the lab can later tell you whether a skin organism was a contaminant. One set means you have thrown away that information permanently.

What the lab tells you, and when

The call comes in stages

Blood cultures report in a sequence, and each stage should change something.

Stage 1Bottle flags positive; Gram stain called to you
Stage 2Rapid identification, often the same day
Stage 3Full identification and susceptibilities

Ask two things when the call comes: how many bottles and how many sets are positive, and how long did it take to turn positive. Both carry interpretive weight that the final report may not display prominently.

What to do next

When a Gram stain result is phoned to you, write down the exact morphology (“gram-positive cocci in clusters,” “gram-negative rods”) and the set/bottle count. Then reassess the patient before you reassess the antibiotics — the bedside exam usually settles contaminant versus pathogen faster than any algorithm.

If any of these grow — even from a single bottle — treat the result as real until proven otherwise and look for a source.

  • Staphylococcus aureus — always significant; triggers a formal management bundle (below).
  • Streptococcus pneumoniae — implies pneumonia, meningitis, or another invasive focus.
  • Enterobacterales (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella, Proteus and relatives) — think urinary tract, biliary tree, or bowel.
  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa — think healthcare exposure, device, neutropenia, or burn/wound.
  • Candida species — candidemia; requires source control, ophthalmologic evaluation in most patients, and specialist input.
  • Streptococcus pyogenes (group A) and Listeria monocytogenes — both invasive pathogens, never bystanders.
Clinical pattern

Gram-negative rods in blood plus flank pain and pyuria points to pyelonephritis with bacteremia. The same Gram stain plus right upper quadrant pain and a rising bilirubin points to ascending cholangitis — a drainage problem, not just a drug problem.

These are common skin and environmental organisms. Growth in one bottle of several, in a patient without hardware and without a compatible illness, is usually a collection artifact.

  • Coagulase-negative staphylococci — the single most common blood culture contaminant.
  • Corynebacterium species (“diphtheroids”).
  • Bacillus species other than Bacillus anthracis.
  • Micrococcus and other skin commensals.
  • Cutibacterium acnes — usually contamination, with the important exception of prosthetic material.
Pitfall

Treating a contaminant is not harmless. It commits a patient to vancomycin, a peripherally inserted central catheter, drug levels, renal monitoring, and days of hospitalization — all for an organism that came off the skin. Before you treat, confirm how many sets grew it and whether the patient has any indwelling hardware.

Advanced pearl

Staphylococcus lugdunensis is a coagulase-negative staphylococcus that behaves clinically like S. aureus, with a genuine propensity for endocarditis and deep infection. When the lab speciates a coagulase-negative staph and names this one, stop applying the contaminant rule.

These require you to think before you dismiss or commit.

  • Viridans group streptococci — frequent oral contaminants, but the classic organisms of subacute endocarditis and a recognized cause of bacteremia in neutropenic patients with mucositis.
  • Enterococcus species — real when they grow, but the meaningful question is the source: urinary tract, abdomen, or endovascular.
  • Anaerobes such as Bacteroides — usually genuine and usually pointing at the gastrointestinal or pelvic tract.
  • Coagulase-negative staph in a patient with a central line, prosthetic valve, or joint — the contaminant rule weakens considerably.
Advanced pearl

Differential time to positivity can help implicate an intravascular catheter: if the catheter-drawn bottle turns positive substantially earlier than a simultaneously drawn peripheral bottle — a threshold of roughly two hours is commonly used — the catheter is likely the source. This requires paired, simultaneous, equal-volume draws, so it only works if you plan for it at the bedside.

The single highest-yield block in this pillar: Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia

If you remember one thing from the entire micro rotation, make it this. S. aureus in a blood culture is never dismissed as a contaminant, and it is never managed as “a positive culture we will cover.” It is a syndrome with a mandatory workup, because untreated or under-treated it seeds valves, spine, and joints.

1
Treat the result as real. Every time.

A single bottle growing gram-positive cocci in clusters that identifies as S. aureus is a true bloodstream infection until proven otherwise. There is no “one of two sets, probably contaminant” version of this organism.

2
Repeat blood cultures to document clearance

Draw follow-up sets — commonly every 48 hours — until they are negative. The date of the first negative culture, not the day therapy started, is what anchors the treatment duration. Bacteremia persisting beyond two to four days on appropriate therapy signals an uncontrolled focus: endocarditis, an infected line, an abscess, or infected hardware.

3
Image the heart

Echocardiography is standard. Transthoracic echocardiography is the usual first study; transesophageal echocardiography is more sensitive and is pursued when the transthoracic study is non-diagnostic or when risk is high — prosthetic valve, implanted cardiac device, persistent bacteremia, or clinical signs of endocarditis. Institutions differ on which patients can be managed with a transthoracic study alone; follow local practice and the consulting service.

4
Find and control the source

Examine the whole patient: every intravascular catheter, every joint, the spine, the skin. Remove infected lines. Drain abscesses. Antibiotics do not sterilize a biofilm on a foreign body or a walled-off collection — source control is the intervention that actually changes outcome. Ask directly about back pain, which may be the only clue to vertebral osteomyelitis or an epidural abscess.

5
Give prolonged intravenous therapy, chosen by susceptibility

Therapy is intravenous, and duration is measured in weeks, not days — longer when the infection is complicated by endocarditis, metastatic foci, or retained hardware. Once susceptibility returns, de-escalate methicillin-susceptible isolates off vancomycin to a beta-lactam such as cefazolin or an antistaphylococcal penicillin, which perform better for susceptible strains.

6
Consult infectious diseases

Formal infectious diseases consultation in S. aureus bacteremia is associated with better adherence to this bundle and with better patient outcomes across multiple published cohorts. Many hospitals now trigger it automatically from the laboratory result.

Cultures clear promptly, no endocarditis, source removed

This behaves as uncomplicated bacteremia: shorter (though still multi-week) intravenous course, counted from the first negative culture, with close follow-up for late metastatic complications.

Cultures stay positive, or a deep focus is found

Complicated bacteremia. Extend therapy substantially, hunt harder for metastatic foci with directed imaging, and escalate source control — this is where cardiac surgery, spine imaging, and repeat drainage decisions get made.

Infective endocarditis, in brief

When bacteremia will not quit

Suspect it when bacteremia is persistent, when the organism is a typical endocarditis pathogen (S. aureus, viridans streptococci, enterococci, and in prosthetic valves coagulase-negative staphylococci), or when the exam changes — a new regurgitant murmur, embolic phenomena in the skin, digits, or brain.

Micro requirementmultiple sets, before antibiotics
ImagingTTE, then TEE if needed
FrameworkModified Duke criteria combine microbiologic, echocardiographic, and clinical findings

Continuous bacteremia is the hallmark: an endovascular focus sheds organisms steadily, which is why multiple sets drawn at separate times all grow the same organism.

Advanced pearl

“Culture-negative endocarditis” is most often the result of antibiotics given before cultures were drawn. When cultures are truly sterile in a compatible clinical picture, the lab can pursue fastidious and intracellular organisms — Coxiella burnetii and Bartonella species are classically sought by serology and molecular testing rather than routine culture. Tell the lab you are considering endocarditis; the workup changes.

Sepsis physiology the lab does not see

Read the patient alongside the plate

Microbiology is slow relative to sepsis. These bedside and chemistry values drive the first six hours while the bottles are still incubating.

Cultures do not delay resuscitation. Draw them fast, then give antimicrobials — in septic shock, the correct sequence is cultures and antibiotics essentially together, not cultures and then a wait.

Why it matters

Every hour of pre-culture antibiotic exposure lowers the chance the lab can name the organism and tell you what it is susceptible to. That is why “cultures before antibiotics” is a real clinical priority and not a lab preference — but it never justifies delaying therapy in a crashing patient.

2. Urinary tract infection

The urinary tract produces more misinterpreted cultures than any other site, because urine is easy to obtain, frequently colonized, and frequently cultured without a reason.

Specimen and what the lab reports

Technique determines meaning

A midstream clean-catch specimen, promptly refrigerated or transported in preservative, is the standard. Urine left at room temperature lets small numbers of organisms multiply into impressive-looking counts.

Preferred specimenmidstream clean-catch
Alternativesstraight catheterization; suprapubic aspirate
Neverurine from a drainage bag or an old catheter
Supporting testurinalysis with microscopy

Pyuria — white cells in the urine — is the marker of inflammation. Its absence in an immunocompetent patient argues strongly against urinary tract infection, which makes urinalysis a useful gate before you even value a culture.

Student pearl

A culture growing three or more organisms with no dominant one is usually a collection problem, not a polymicrobial infection. The right move is to recollect properly, not to treat everything that grew.

Asymptomatic bacteriuria

The concept that prevents the most harm

Asymptomatic bacteriuria is a positive urine culture in a patient without urinary symptoms. It is common — in older adults, in patients with diabetes, and in essentially everyone with a long-term catheter — and in general it should not be treated.

The well-established exceptions where screening and treatment are recommended:

  • Pregnancy.
  • Before urologic procedures in which mucosal bleeding is anticipated.

Outside these, treating bacteriuria yields no benefit and buys adverse drug effects, Clostridioides difficile infection, and resistance.

Pitfall

The confused older adult with a “positive urine culture.” Delirium alone, without urinary symptoms or another sign pointing at the urinary tract, is weak evidence for urinary tract infection — and bacteriuria is extremely common in this population at baseline. Anchoring on the urine culture is how pneumonia, medication effects, stroke, hypoxia, and metabolic derangement get missed. Look for the real cause first; the culture will still be there.

Catheters change everything

Colonization is the default state

Every long-term indwelling urinary catheter is colonized, and a biofilm forms on the catheter surface. A culture from that catheter reports the biofilm, not necessarily the bladder.

  • Do not perform surveillance cultures on catheterized patients who feel well.
  • Culture only when there is a genuine clinical reason to suspect infection.
  • When infection is suspected and the catheter has been in place a while, replace the catheter and collect the specimen from the new one.
  • Remove catheters that no longer have an indication — the most effective prevention there is.
What to do next

Before ordering a urine culture on any inpatient, ask yourself out loud: what urinary symptom or finding am I explaining, and what will I do differently with each possible result? If you cannot answer, the order is not indicated.

Cystitis versus pyelonephritis

Why drug choice diverges

Cystitis is a bladder mucosal infection: dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic discomfort, no fever. Pyelonephritis involves renal parenchyma: fever, flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, systemic illness, and a real risk of bacteremia.

CystitisAgents that concentrate in urine are adequate
PyelonephritisRequires adequate tissue and serum levels
Pyelonephritis workupurine culture + blood cultures
Do not confuse

Agents such as nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin achieve high concentrations in urine but not in renal tissue or blood. They are reasonable for uncomplicated cystitis and are not adequate for pyelonephritis or bacteremic urinary infection. A susceptibility report showing “S” tells you the organism is inhibited at achievable urinary concentrations — it does not tell you the drug reaches the kidney.

Advanced pearl

Two organism-specific signals worth knowing. Candida in the urine is usually colonization, particularly with a catheter, and rarely warrants antifungal therapy on its own. S. aureus in the urine is unusual as a primary urinary pathogen and should prompt you to consider hematogenous seeding of the kidney from a bloodstream source — draw blood cultures.

3. Pneumonia

Pneumonia is where specimen quality matters most, because the only route to the lower airway passes through a heavily colonized mouth.

Community-acquired pneumonia develops outside healthcare exposure, so the likely organisms are the ones that colonize and invade normal upper airways.

  • Streptococcus pneumoniae — still the classic bacterial cause.
  • Haemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis — more common with underlying lung disease.
  • Atypicals: Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Legionella species. These do not grow on routine culture media and will never appear on a standard sputum culture — they require directed testing.
  • Respiratory viruses: influenza, SARS-CoV-2, respiratory syncytial virus and others, which can cause pneumonia directly or precede bacterial superinfection.

In outpatients with mild community-acquired pneumonia, microbiologic testing often does not change management and is frequently omitted. Testing intensity should scale with severity.

Hospital-acquired pneumonia is defined by onset after a period of hospitalization — commonly counted from 48 hours after admission — and ventilator-associated pneumonia after that interval on mechanical ventilation. Airway flora shifts within days of admission, which is why the organism list changes.

  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa and other non-fermenting gram-negative rods.
  • Enterobacterales, including strains with acquired beta-lactamases.
  • Staphylococcus aureus, including methicillin-resistant strains.
  • Acinetobacter species in some institutions.

Empiric coverage in this setting should be informed by your own unit's antibiogram — the local resistance picture, not a textbook list, is what predicts adequacy.

Clinical pattern

An endotracheal aspirate from a ventilated patient almost always grows something. Growth alone is not pneumonia. Pair the culture with new or worsening infiltrate, worsening gas exchange, fever, and purulent secretions before you call it a ventilator-associated pneumonia.

A negative Legionella urinary antigen does not exclude legionellosis — non-serogroup-1 L. pneumophila and other Legionella species are missed. If clinical suspicion is real, ask the lab about additional testing such as culture on selective media or molecular assays, and say the word “Legionella” explicitly on the requisition, because the required media are not part of a routine setup.

Specimen quality: saliva is not sputum

The Gram stain screen

Before culturing an expectorated specimen, many labs screen the Gram stain for host cells. Abundant squamous epithelial cells mean the specimen came from the mouth; the specimen may be rejected outright with a comment asking for recollection.

NeutrophilsMarker of lower respiratory inflammation
CutoffsVary by laboratory; screening criteria are institution-specific

A rejection comment is not the lab being difficult. It is the lab telling you that culturing that specimen would generate a confidently wrong answer.

Advanced pearl

Lower respiratory sampling can be quantitative. Bronchoalveolar lavage and protected specimen brush cultures are reported with colony counts, and laboratories apply thresholds to separate probable infection from airway colonization. The exact thresholds and whether quantitative culture is offered at all vary substantially between institutions.

The MRSA nares screen, used correctly

A de-escalation tool

A nasal swab tested for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus by PCR or culture is a colonization test. Its value in pneumonia lies almost entirely in its high negative predictive value.

Screen negativeMRSA pneumonia is unlikely; supports stopping vancomycin
Screen positiveColonized — does not by itself prove MRSA pneumonia

Used well, this test shortens vancomycin exposure across an entire hospital. Used backwards — as a reason to start anti-MRSA therapy in a colonized patient with no other evidence — it does the opposite.

What to do next

On any patient started empirically on vancomycin for pneumonia, check whether a MRSA nares screen has resulted. A negative screen in a patient who is improving is one of the most concrete antimicrobial stewardship actions a student can suggest on rounds.

Pitfall

Treating what the airway was already carrying. Two versions recur. First, chasing an upper-airway colonizer reported from a poor-quality sputum — normal respiratory flora and oral organisms will grow if you culture the mouth. Second, and more absolute: Candida grown from a respiratory specimen is essentially never true pneumonia in an immunocompetent host. Candida colonizes airways, especially in ventilated patients on antibiotics. Treating it with an antifungal exposes the patient to toxicity and cost for no benefit and, worse, stops the search for the real pathogen.

4. Meningitis and central nervous system infection

Cerebrospinal fluid is the most precious specimen in the hospital: it is obtained by an invasive procedure, the volume is small, and it cannot be casually repeated. Order everything you need the first time.

What to send on every lumbar puncture

Do not lose a tube to an afterthought
Tube for hematologycell count with differential
Tube for chemistryglucose and protein
Tube for microbiologyGram stain and culture
Also recordopening pressure; paired serum glucose

If tuberculous or fungal meningitis is a consideration, the lab needs substantially more volume — mycobacterial and fungal recovery are volume-dependent. Say so before the needle comes out, not after.

Student pearl

Cerebrospinal fluid glucose is interpreted against a simultaneous serum glucose, not against a fixed number. A cerebrospinal fluid glucose of 60 mg/dL looks normal until you learn the serum glucose was 400 mg/dL — the ratio is what matters.

Do not wait

The sequencing rule

In suspected bacterial meningitis, antibiotics are time-critical. The correct sequence when imaging is required before lumbar puncture:

  • Draw blood cultures.
  • Give dexamethasone and empiric antimicrobials.
  • Obtain imaging.
  • Perform the lumbar puncture.

Cerebrospinal fluid cell count, protein, and glucose remain informative after antibiotics, and blood cultures plus molecular testing frequently still identify the organism even when the cerebrospinal fluid culture is sterilized.

Pitfall

Delaying antibiotics to preserve the culture. This is the error that kills patients in bacterial meningitis. A sterile culture in a living patient is a far better outcome than a positive culture in a patient who deteriorated during a computed tomography scan. Never let specimen purity outrank timely therapy.

The classic bacterial pattern: a very high white cell count with neutrophil predominance, a low glucose, and a high protein. The low glucose is the discriminating feature students most often forget.

Typical organisms depend on age and host: Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis across most of adult life; group B Streptococcus, Escherichia coli, and Listeria monocytogenes in neonates; Listeria again at the older extreme and in impaired cell-mediated immunity.

The aseptic pattern: a moderate pleocytosis with lymphocyte predominance, a normal glucose, and a normal or mildly elevated protein. Enteroviruses are the most common cause; herpes simplex virus is the one you cannot afford to miss because it causes treatable encephalitis.

Do not confuse

Very early viral meningitis can be neutrophil-predominant, and partially treated bacterial meningitis can look deceptively bland. The cell differential alone never settles the question — read it together with the glucose, the protein, the Gram stain, and above all how sick the patient is.

The indolent pattern: lymphocytic pleocytosis, low glucose, and a very high protein, evolving over days to weeks rather than hours. Tuberculous meningitis and cryptococcal meningitis are the archetypes.

  • Send large-volume cerebrospinal fluid for mycobacterial and fungal culture; small aliquots lower sensitivity.
  • Cryptococcal antigen testing on cerebrospinal fluid and serum is sensitive and fast; request it directly when the host is immunocompromised.
  • Mycobacterial cultures take weeks — start empiric therapy on clinical grounds when the picture fits.

Empiric coverage varies by age and host. The recurring principles rather than a memorized regimen:

  • Cover the age-appropriate typical bacteria, with an agent that penetrates the central nervous system at meningeal dosing.
  • Add ampicillin at the extremes of age — neonates and older adults — and in impaired cell-mediated immunity, to cover Listeria monocytogenes, which is not covered by cephalosporins.
  • Add coverage for resistant pneumococcus per local practice.
  • Add acyclovir when herpes simplex encephalitis is a consideration.
  • Dexamethasone is given in suspected pneumococcal meningitis in adults, ideally before or with the first antibiotic dose.
  • Neurosurgical patients and those with shunts or recent trauma need coverage for staphylococci and healthcare-associated gram-negative rods.
Advanced pearl

Multiplex meningitis/encephalitis PCR panels return many targets in about an hour, which is genuinely useful. The limitations matter just as much. They test a fixed menu — anything not on the panel is invisible, including mycobacteria. False negatives occur, particularly at low organism burden. False positives occur, and their consequences are worst when pretest probability is low, which is why some institutions restrict ordering to patients with abnormal cerebrospinal fluid. And the panel gives no susceptibility data. It supplements culture; it does not replace it.

5. Skin and soft tissue infection

This is the syndrome where the most useful micro decision is frequently to send nothing at all, and where the definitive treatment is often a scalpel rather than a drug.

Non-purulent cellulitis

Usually streptococcal

Spreading erythema, warmth, and tenderness without drainage or a collection. Beta-hemolytic streptococci predominate.

  • Routine culture is not indicated in uncomplicated cellulitis.
  • A swab of intact skin samples surface flora and answers nothing.
  • Blood cultures are low yield in uncomplicated disease; reserve them for systemic toxicity, immunocompromise, or unusual exposures.
Student pearl

Cellulitis is a clinical diagnosis. The most valuable thing you can add is a marked border and a time, so tomorrow's team can tell progression from resolution.

Purulent lesions and abscess

Drainage is the therapy

Furuncle, carbuncle, or abscess. Staphylococcus aureus dominates, and methicillin resistance is common enough in many communities to matter.

  • Incision and drainage is the treatment; antibiotics are adjunctive.
  • Send the pus or the abscess wall — aspirated material or tissue, not a swab dragged across the surface.
  • The culture guides therapy and feeds the local antibiogram; both are worth the extra minute.
Why it matters

Antibiotics do not penetrate an undrained collection meaningfully. Escalating the drug while leaving pus in place is the commonest reason a soft tissue infection “fails therapy.”

Necrotizing infection

A surgical emergency

Pain out of proportion to findings, rapid progression, systemic toxicity, skin changes such as bullae, dusky discoloration, or crepitus, and firm induration extending beyond visible erythema.

  • Call surgery immediately. Do not wait for imaging, a score, or a culture.
  • Give broad antimicrobials and resuscitate in parallel. Empiric regimens conventionally add a protein-synthesis inhibitor such as clindamycin or linezolid for toxin suppression when group A streptococcal or clostridial infection is possible.
  • The diagnostic and therapeutic act is operative exploration; deep tissue from the operating room is the specimen that matters.
Pitfall

Treating necrotizing infection as a microbiology question. Time to debridement drives survival. If the thought crosses your mind, the phone call to surgery comes before anything you do with a specimen container.

Diabetic foot infection and chronic wounds

Chronic open wounds are colonized by definition. A superficial swab reliably returns a list of organisms living on the surface, and that list correlates poorly with the organisms causing deep infection.

  • Do not base therapy on a superficial wound swab.
  • Do obtain deep tissue at debridement, or bone specimens when osteomyelitis is suspected.
  • Clinical infection is judged by inflammation, drainage, and systemic signs — not by whether a swab grew something.
  • Probing to bone and imaging findings raise concern for underlying osteomyelitis, which changes the specimen you need and the duration of therapy.
Do not confuse

Colonization, contamination, and infection. Colonization means organisms live there without causing harm; contamination means they entered during collection; infection means they are causing tissue damage. Only the third one is a reason to treat, and only the patient — not the report — can tell you which you are looking at.

6. Bone and joint infection, including prosthetic joints

Deep, closed-space infections where getting the specimen right is nearly the whole diagnostic exercise — and where the wrong specimen produces a confident, wrong answer that gets treated for six weeks.

Septic arthritis

Aspirate the joint

Synovial fluid is the specimen. Send it for cell count with differential, crystal examination, Gram stain, and culture — all four, from the same tap. In a sexually active young adult, particularly with a migratory or oligoarticular presentation, tenosynovitis, or a pustular rash, consider disseminated gonococcal infection: synovial Gram stain and culture are often negative because the organism is fastidious, so send nucleic acid amplification testing from genital and extragenital sites and tell the laboratory that Neisseria gonorrhoeae is under consideration.

Synovial WBC78,000 /mcL Neutrophils94% Crystalsnone seen Gram stainGPC in clusters

Very high, neutrophil-predominant synovial counts favor bacterial infection, but the ranges overlap with crystal arthropathy, so no single count rules infection in or out.

Do not confuse

Finding crystals does not exclude infection. Gout and septic arthritis can occupy the same joint at the same time. If the clinical picture is convincing, treat and drain regardless of the crystal result.

Osteomyelitis and prosthetic joint infection

Sample the bone, not the tract

The reference specimen is bone or periprosthetic tissue, obtained surgically or by image-guided biopsy, ideally with the patient off antibiotics when it is safe to hold them.

Cutibacterium acnes — a slow-growing anaerobic gram-positive rod, classically implicated in shoulder arthroplasty infection — will be missed on standard incubation times. Tell the lab you are working up a prosthetic joint infection so the cultures are held long enough.

Pitfall

The sinus tract swab. Swabbing a draining sinus over infected bone samples the tract's own colonizing flora, and it correlates poorly with the organism actually infecting the bone. Committing a patient to weeks of therapy based on a sinus tract swab is a well-described way to treat the wrong organism for a long time.

Advanced pearl

Adjuncts used at some centers include sonication of explanted hardware to dislodge biofilm organisms before culture, synovial fluid biomarkers, and molecular testing on tissue. Availability and interpretation are institution-specific, and orthopedic and infectious diseases services generally coordinate their use.

7. Gastrointestinal infection and Clostridioides difficile

Stool testing is where indiscriminate ordering causes the most downstream harm, because the gut is full of organisms and modern assays are exquisitely sensitive.

Clostridioides difficile: test the right patient, then read the result correctly

  • Test only unformed, diarrheal stool. Many labs reject formed stool outright, because in a patient who is passing formed stool the result almost never represents C. difficile disease. The exception is fulminant disease with ileus or toxic megacolon, where diarrhea may be absent altogether — in that patient call the laboratory rather than assuming testing is impossible.
  • Do not test a patient receiving laxatives — a common cause of a positive result in a patient whose diarrhea is iatrogenic.
  • Do not repeat testing to confirm cure. Assays stay positive after clinical resolution; a follow-up test cannot tell you that treatment worked.
  • Do not immediately repeat a negative test in the same illness episode — the added yield is small and false positives accumulate.
Why it matters

Asymptomatic C. difficile colonization is common, especially among hospitalized patients. Because the organism is often already present, the decision to send the test is the decision that determines whether you will be misled — not the assay itself.

Two assays, two different questions

The interpretive core
NAAT (nucleic acid amplification)Detects the organism or toxin gene — presence, not activity
Toxin immunoassayDetects the toxin itself — correlates better with active disease
GDH antigenSensitive screen for the organism; not specific for toxin production

A nucleic acid amplification test can be positive in a colonized patient whose diarrhea has another cause. A toxin immunoassay is more specific for disease but less sensitive. Neither is “the right test” in isolation.

Many laboratories therefore use a two-step algorithm: a sensitive screen followed by a toxin assay, with a molecular test to arbitrate discordant results. The specific algorithm, and how results are worded on the report, vary meaningfully between institutions — learn how your own lab reports it.

Clinical pattern

Screen-positive but toxin-negative, in a patient with several loose stools who just started a laxative and has no leukocytosis: most consistent with colonization. The same result in a patient with profuse diarrhea, a rising white count, abdominal distension, and recent broad-spectrum antibiotics: treat.

Everything else in the stool

Panels, the 3-day rule, and parasites

Stool pathogen panels detect a broad set of bacterial, viral, and parasitic targets in hours. They are excellent for acute community-onset diarrhea and dramatically outperform culture for yield — but they detect nucleic acid, so they can be positive with recent or resolving infection and with carriage.

The “3-day rule”: routine stool culture is rarely useful for diarrhea beginning more than about three days after hospital admission. In that patient the differential is C. difficile, medications, and enteral feeding — not community foodborne pathogens. Many labs decline such cultures for this reason.

Think about parasites with persistent or chronic diarrhea, relevant travel or exposure history, waterborne outbreaks, or an immunocompromised host. Antigen or molecular testing for Giardia and Cryptosporidium generally outperforms routine microscopic ova and parasite examination for those two, and the classic “three specimens on separate days” advice applies to microscopy.

Advanced pearl

When Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli is detected — by toxin assay or panel — two things follow. Antimicrobial therapy is generally avoided because of concern for precipitating hemolytic uremic syndrome, and the laboratory works to recover the isolate for public health, since a molecular result alone does not support outbreak investigation. Culture still has jobs that molecular testing cannot do.

8. Intra-abdominal infection

Expect a crowd

Polymicrobial by nature

Secondary peritonitis follows a breach in the gastrointestinal tract, so the infecting flora is gut flora: Enterobacterales, anaerobes such as Bacteroides fragilis, streptococci, and often enterococci; Candida appears in some patients, particularly after upper gastrointestinal perforation or prior broad antimicrobial exposure.

Why it matters

Source control dominates. Percutaneous drainage of an abscess, repair of a perforation, relief of an obstructed biliary tree — these determine outcome far more than the choice among reasonable antibiotic regimens. If an intra-abdominal infection is not improving, the first question is not “is the drug broad enough?” but “is the source actually controlled?”

Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis

The instructive exception

In a patient with cirrhosis and ascites, infection can arise in the ascitic fluid without a perforation. Diagnostic paracentesis is the test, and the cell count is what makes the diagnosis.

An ascitic fluid polymorphonuclear count at or above 250 cells/mm3 supports the diagnosis and prompts treatment, even when the culture is negative — and cultures are frequently negative unless fluid is inoculated into blood culture bottles at the bedside.

Clinical pattern

Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis is usually caused by a single organism. A polymicrobial ascitic fluid culture should raise concern for secondary peritonitis — a perforated viscus — which is a surgical problem, not an antibiotic problem.

9. Two short but important cases

Genital and sexually transmitted infection

Mostly molecular now

Diagnosis has shifted almost entirely to nucleic acid amplification testing, which is more sensitive than culture and works on patient-friendly specimens including urine and self-collected swabs.

  • Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis — NAAT is the standard. Extragenital (pharyngeal and rectal) sites must be specifically requested; a urine test does not cover them.
  • Culture still matters for gonorrhea when susceptibility information is needed, such as suspected treatment failure — molecular tests do not report susceptibility, and antimicrobial resistance in this organism is actively monitored by public health agencies.
  • Syphilis is diagnosed serologically, using treponemal and non-treponemal tests in a defined sequence; laboratories differ in which test they run first.
  • Herpes simplex virus — PCR of a lesion swab, which outperforms viral culture.
  • Trichomonas vaginalis — molecular testing is far more sensitive than wet mount microscopy.
Pitfall

Ordering a test of cure too early. Residual nucleic acid from non-viable organisms can keep a NAAT positive for a period after successful treatment, so a test repeated too soon can look like failure when the patient is cured. Follow the recommended retesting interval, and remember that retesting for reinfection months later is a different and often more valuable action.

Febrile neutropenia and the immunocompromised host

Fewer signs, wider differential, lower threshold

Without neutrophils, the usual signs of infection are muted: less pus, a quieter chest film, less pyuria despite genuine infection. Fever may be the only finding, and it is treated as an emergency.

  • Draw blood cultures from a peripheral site and from each lumen of any central catheter.
  • Start broad empiric therapy with antipseudomonal coverage promptly — delays measured in hours matter.
  • With prolonged neutropenia, add invasive fungal disease to the differential: Candida species and molds such as Aspergillus.
  • Adjunctive fungal markers — galactomannan and beta-D-glucan — are used alongside imaging and culture. Both have meaningful false-positive and false-negative rates and are interpreted in clinical context, not alone.
  • Chest computed tomography detects findings a plain radiograph misses in these patients.
Advanced pearl

The differential broadens in a direction that tracks the specific immune defect: neutropenia predisposes to bacteria and molds; impaired cell-mediated immunity to intracellular pathogens including Listeria, mycobacteria, Cryptococcus, and viruses such as cytomegalovirus; impaired humoral immunity and asplenia to encapsulated organisms including Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Neisseria meningitidis. Naming the defect narrows the workup faster than any panel.

Loose ends worth knowing

The culture is negative but the patient clearly has an infection. What happened?

Work through the list in order. Antibiotics were given before the specimen was collected — by far the most common explanation. The organism does not grow on routine media (Legionella, Mycoplasma, mycobacteria, Bartonella, many fungi) and no one told the lab to look. The specimen sampled the wrong compartment — a swab where tissue was needed. The specimen volume was too small, which matters enormously for blood, cerebrospinal fluid, and fungal or mycobacterial cultures. Transport was delayed or the wrong medium was used, which is fatal to fastidious organisms and anaerobes. Or the syndrome is not infectious at all. Call the laboratory and describe the clinical picture; the workup can often be extended on the specimen already in hand.

How do I decide whether a growing organism is the pathogen?

Four questions. Is this site normally sterile? Blood, cerebrospinal fluid, joint fluid, and deep tissue carry a much higher prior probability that growth is real. Is this organism a known pathogen at this site? Candida in blood is alarming; Candida in sputum is background. Is the quantity and consistency compelling? Multiple sets, pure heavy growth, and reproducibility across specimens all argue for a true pathogen. Does the patient's illness match? If the clinical syndrome does not fit the organism, distrust the organism before you distrust the patient.

Why does it matter what I write on the requisition?

Because it determines what the laboratory actually does. Suspected Legionella, Nocardia, mycobacteria, fungi, Vibrio, or a prosthetic joint infection all require different media, different stains, longer incubation, or a different biosafety posture — none of which happen by default on a routine order. Clinical history on the requisition is not paperwork; it is the instruction set. It also protects laboratory staff, since some organisms require specific handling once suspected.

A rapid molecular test and the culture disagree. Which do I believe?

Neither automatically. Molecular tests detect nucleic acid, which can persist from dead organisms and can be present in colonization; they are limited to the targets on their menu, and they usually give no susceptibility information. Culture requires viable organisms and is defeated by prior antibiotics, but it yields an isolate for susceptibility testing and for public health work. When they disagree, the tiebreaker is the patient. Then call the laboratory — discordance is often explainable, and the microbiologist has seen the pattern before.

What should I actually do with a result at 2 a.m.?

Ask three things in sequence. Does this change therapy right now? A Gram stain showing gram-positive cocci in clusters from blood does; a final identification on an improving patient at hour 60 usually does not. Does this change the plan for tomorrow? Write it down for rounds rather than acting reflexively overnight. Does this suggest I have the wrong diagnosis? That is the most valuable thing a culture can tell you, and the easiest to miss when you are tired and only checking whether the drug covers the bug.

What to do next

On your next patient with a suspected infection, write four lines in your note before you order anything: the syndrome you think this is, the compartment you need to sample, the result that would change your management, and the pitfall for that syndrome. Do that consistently and you will order fewer tests, interpret them better, and be the person on the team who notices when the culture is answering a question nobody asked.

Wrap-up · Take it to the floor

Final recap — what to carry onto service.

If you remember nothing else from today, remember these. They are enough to read a micro report intelligently and to avoid the mistakes that actually harm patients.

  1. The specimen decides the answer. Right site, right container, ideally before antibiotics — and tissue or fluid beats a swab almost every time.
  2. The central question in all of microbiology is contamination vs colonization vs infection. Ask it of every positive result.
  3. The Gram stain gives you a usable answer in minutes: purple or pink, cocci or rods, clusters or chains.
  4. A sputum sample full of squamous epithelial cells is saliva, not lung — the lab may reject it, and you should not treat it.
  5. Blood cultures: two sets, adequate volume, before antibiotics. Volume is the single biggest driver of yield in adults.
  6. S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, Enterobacterales and Candida in a blood culture are essentially never dismissed as contaminants. Coagulase-negative staph in one of several bottles usually is.
  7. Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia demands a bundle: repeat cultures to document clearance, echocardiography, source control, intravenous therapy, and an infectious diseases consult.
  8. MALDI-TOF identifies the organism; it does not tell you susceptibility. Those are two different questions and two different waits.
  9. Molecular tests detect nucleic acid — fast and sensitive, but they can flag dead or merely colonizing organisms, and they only find what is on the panel.
  10. Never compare MICs between different antibiotics. Each drug has its own breakpoint; a lower number is not a stronger drug.
  11. S, I and R are predictions tied to breakpoints and usual dosing at the usual site — not measures of potency.
  12. Know the intrinsic resistance traps: enterococci are not treated with cephalosporins, Listeria is not covered by cephalosporins, and organisms without a cell wall are untouched by beta-lactams.
  13. Recognize MRSA, VRE, ESBL, AmpC and CRE by name — and treat carbapenem-resistant organisms as both a therapeutic problem and an infection-control one.
  14. An “S” is necessary but not sufficient: the drug still has to reach the site (daptomycin is inactivated in the lung), and no antibiotic substitutes for source control.
  15. Asymptomatic bacteriuria is the most over-treated finding in the hospital. Do not culture urine without urinary symptoms; pregnancy and pre-urologic procedures are the well-established exceptions.
  16. Test for Clostridioides difficile only on unformed stool, only when it will change management, and never as a test of cure.
  17. Reassess at 48–72 hours when the data return: de-escalate to the narrowest effective agent, switch intravenous to oral when appropriate, and set a stop date.

How to sound smart on rounds

  • She has no urinary symptoms — I would not send that culture.
  • That is one of four bottles with coagulase-negative staph; it looks like a contaminant.
  • S. aureus in the blood — can we get repeat cultures, an echo, and ID on board?
  • Cultures are back and it is a pan-susceptible E. coli. Can we come off the carbapenem?
  • The MRSA nares screen is negative, so I think we can stop the vancomycin.
  • Before we escalate antibiotics — is there a source we still need to drain?

Good questions to ask on service

  • What does our local antibiogram say for this organism here?
  • Were these cultures drawn before the first dose of antibiotics?
  • Is this a true pathogen, or is it this patient’s normal flora?
  • Do we have source control, or is there still something to drain or remove?
  • Now that we have an identification, what is the narrowest agent that works?
  • Is this an organism with an inducible AmpC we should worry about?
