# Clinical Microbiology — One-Day Crash Course
## Phase 1 Planning Package

> For medical students on a single-day clinical microbiology rotation.
> Author/educator: academic pathologist.

This document is the planning and content blueprint that the dashboard (`index.html`) is built from. It is written so the dashboard could be rebuilt or extended without guessing.

---

## A. Learner Profile

| Dimension | Assumption |
|---|---|
| **Audience** | Medical students, single-day rotation in clinical microbiology |
| **Baseline knowledge** | Almost no exposure to how a micro lab works; may never have seen a Gram stain read |
| **Existing strengths** | Solid clinical framework — sepsis, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, meningitis, cellulitis; comfortable with "the patient looks septic" |
| **Cognitive goal** | Read a micro report intelligently, and tell **infection** apart from **colonization** and **contamination** |
| **Failure mode to avoid** | Drowning novices in taxonomy and biochemical identification tables before they have a clinical scaffold |
| **Design implication** | Clinical language first → lab framework second → specialist detail explicitly flagged as "Advanced pearl" |

**Teaching stance:** scaffold simple → complex, explain every abbreviation on first use, prioritize pattern recognition, and always connect the laboratory result to a bedside decision.

## B. Learning Objectives (high-yield, by pillar)

### Pillar 1 — Specimens & the diagnostic toolbox
1. Explain what the clinical micro lab can and cannot answer, and why the specimen determines the answer.
2. Distinguish **contamination, colonization, and infection**, with examples.
3. Read a Gram stain report (Gram reaction, morphology, arrangement, host cells) and say what it changes.
4. Describe realistic turnaround times: Gram stain in minutes, growth in ~12–24 h, identification and susceptibility typically ~48–72 h total.
5. Order and interpret blood cultures — two sets, adequate volume, drawn before antibiotics — and judge a likely contaminant.
6. State what MALDI-TOF and molecular panels each add, and their key limitations.
7. Apply **diagnostic stewardship**: recognize the tests that should not be sent.

### Pillar 2 — Reading the organisms
1. Use the Gram stain as a decision tree to generate a clinically useful shortlist.
2. Separate *Staphylococcus aureus* from coagulase-negative staphylococci and state why it matters.
3. Use hemolysis and clinical context to place the streptococci and enterococci.
4. Recognize the major Gram-negative groups: Enterobacterales vs non-fermenters (especially *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*).
5. Say when to suspect anaerobes.
6. Recognize the organisms that do not follow Gram-stain rules (Mycoplasma, Legionella, Chlamydia, mycobacteria).
7. Interpret common fungal isolates — above all, that candidemia is always significant while *Candida* in sputum is not.

### Pillar 3 — Susceptibility, resistance & stewardship *(highest-risk pillar)*
1. Define the **minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)** and explain why MICs must never be compared between different drugs.
2. Interpret **S / I / R** as breakpoint-based predictions, not measures of potency.
3. Use a local **antibiogram** to guide empiric therapy.
4. Recognize **intrinsic resistance** traps.
5. Recognize by name: **MRSA, VRE, ESBL, AmpC, CRE**, and multidrug-resistant *Pseudomonas*.
6. Explain why an organism reported "S" can still fail — site penetration, and the absence of source control.
7. Describe the stewardship workflow: empiric → cultures → reassess at 48–72 h → de-escalate → IV-to-oral → stop date.

### Pillar 4 — The syndrome-based approach
1. For each major syndrome, name the correct specimen and the expected organisms.
2. Manage a positive blood culture, and recite the **_S. aureus_ bacteremia bundle**.
3. Distinguish **asymptomatic bacteriuria** from urinary tract infection and resist treating the former.
4. Judge sputum quality; use pneumococcal/Legionella urinary antigen and the MRSA nares screen appropriately.
5. Interpret **cerebrospinal fluid** patterns (bacterial vs viral vs tuberculous/fungal) and never delay antibiotics for testing.
6. Culture the right thing in skin, soft-tissue, bone and joint infection — tissue, not a surface swab.
7. Test for *Clostridioides difficile* correctly, and interpret NAAT vs toxin.

## D. Core Content Map (must know / should recognize / advanced-optional)

### Pillar 1 — Specimens & diagnostics
- **Must know:** contamination vs colonization vs infection; Gram stain basics; two blood culture sets before antibiotics; MALDI-TOF identifies but does not give susceptibility; don't culture urine without symptoms.
- **Should recognize:** specimen quality (squamous cells = saliva); realistic turnaround times; what molecular panels do and miss; antigen tests.
- **Advanced / optional:** specific media formulations, 16S rRNA sequencing workflows, time-to-positivity thresholds, biochemical identification schemes.

### Pillar 2 — Organisms
- **Must know:** the Gram stain decision tree; *S. aureus* vs coagulase-negative staph; strep hemolysis groups; Enterobacterales vs *Pseudomonas*; candidemia vs *Candida* colonization.
- **Should recognize:** when to suspect anaerobes; the atypicals and why beta-lactams fail against Mycoplasma; mycobacteria are slow and acid-fast; *Listeria* at the extremes of age.
- **Advanced / optional:** full biochemical panels, serotyping, *S. lugdunensis*, Lemierre syndrome, endemic mycoses in depth.

### Pillar 3 — Susceptibility & stewardship
- **Must know:** MIC definition; never compare MICs across drugs; S/I/R meaning; intrinsic resistance traps; MRSA/VRE/ESBL/AmpC/CRE by name; source control.
- **Should recognize:** the antibiogram; selective/cascade reporting; D-test; MRSA nares screen negative predictive value; de-escalation and IV-to-oral.
- **Advanced / optional:** specific breakpoint values, carbapenemase gene classes in detail, newer beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor selection, PK/PD target attainment.

### Pillar 4 — Syndromes
- **Must know:** *S. aureus* bacteremia bundle; asymptomatic bacteriuria; sputum quality; CSF patterns and not delaying antibiotics; drain the abscess; test C. diff only on unformed stool.
- **Should recognize:** CAP vs HAP/VAP; catheter-associated urinary issues; prosthetic joint infection and prolonged cultures; polymicrobial intra-abdominal infection.
- **Advanced / optional:** endocarditis diagnostic criteria in full, detailed empiric regimens by institution, febrile neutropenia risk stratification.

## E. Clinical-Language-First Translation

Every technical concept is framed with three lenses:

- **Why it matters** — the clinical stake (missed sepsis, unnecessary antibiotics, resistance, *C. difficile*).
- **When it comes up** — the real situation (a 2 a.m. positive blood culture, a pre-op urine, a confused elderly patient).
- **What to do next** — the interpretation or action expected of a trainee.

Example (MIC): *Why it matters* — students routinely conclude that the drug with the lower MIC is "stronger" and pick the wrong agent. *When it comes up* — every susceptibility report. *What to do next* — read each drug against **its own** breakpoint (S/I/R), then choose on site of infection, spectrum, and toxicity — never on the raw number.

## F. Resource-Gathering Plan (source *types* — no fabricated citations)

Content is synthesized from standard, well-established teaching knowledge. For verification and future updates, consult these **real, named bodies** (specific editions/links to be added by the educator; none are invented here):

- **CLSI** — *M100 Performance Standards for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing* (breakpoints, D-test, reporting).
- **EUCAST** — European breakpoint tables and expert rules.
- **ASM** (American Society for Microbiology) — *Manual of Clinical Microbiology*; *Clinical Microbiology Procedures Handbook*.
- **IDSA** (Infectious Diseases Society of America) — syndrome guidelines (bloodstream/endocarditis, urinary tract infection, pneumonia, meningitis, skin and soft tissue, *C. difficile*, candidiasis, prosthetic joint infection).
- **IDSA/ASM** joint *Guide to Utilization of the Microbiology Laboratory for Diagnosis of Infectious Diseases* — the definitive specimen-selection reference.
- **CDC** — antimicrobial resistance threat reports, NHSN surveillance definitions, healthcare infection control guidance.
- **CAP** (College of American Pathologists) — laboratory accreditation and reporting standards.
- **SHEA/IDSA** — antimicrobial stewardship and diagnostic stewardship guidance.

> Labeled honestly: these are **source types to consult**, named accurately. The dashboard UI deliberately contains **no fabricated citations, DOIs, or invented breakpoint values**.

### 4. Recommended Teaching Flow for the Day
1. **Orient (5 min):** the four-pillar map and the contamination/colonization/infection question.
2. **Walk the specimen path (45 min):** Pillar 1 — ideally alongside a real bench tour.
3. **Read organisms (50 min):** Pillar 2 — work the Gram stain decision tree out loud.
4. **Susceptibility (50 min):** Pillar 3 — slow down on MIC comparison and the "S but wrong drug" trap.
5. **Syndromes (45 min):** Pillar 4 — anchor to patients currently on the service.
6. **Integrate (30 min):** Case Lab.
7. **Self-test (20 min):** Quiz Center; review misses together.
8. **Consolidate (10 min):** Recap and rounds pearls.
